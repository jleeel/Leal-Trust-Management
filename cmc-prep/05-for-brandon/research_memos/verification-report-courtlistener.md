## PRIORITY 1 — Dissociation / buyout (Corp. Code §§ 16601, 16603, 16701, 16103)

### Rappaport v. Gelfand (2011) 197 Cal.App.4th 1213 — **VERIFIED (read operative passages)**
CL cluster 5810457, opinion 5665898 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5810457/rappaport-v-gelfand/). 2d Dist., Croskey, J. Bench trial to determine the "buyout price" of a dissociating law-firm partner under § 16701(b); described in the opinion as "this apparent case of first impression in California."

**Quotes § 16701(a)–(b) verbatim, including the interest sentence** (at pp. 1217, 1225–1226):
> "(a) If a partner is dissociated from a partnership, the partnership shall cause the dissociated partner's interest in the partnership to be purchased for a buyout price determined pursuant to subdivision (b). [¶] (b) The buyout price of a dissociated partner's interest is the amount that would have been distributable to the dissociating partner under subdivision (b) of Section 16807 if, on the date of dissociation, the assets of the partnership were sold at a price equal to the greater of the liquidation value or the value based on a sale of the entire business as a going concern without the dissociated partner and the partnership was wound up as of that date. **Interest shall be paid from the date of dissociation to the date of payment.**"

**Interest from dissociation date applied in the judgment** (at p. 1223): "the trial court entered judgment in favor of Rappaport for his 31 percent share of the partnership in the sum of $230,758 **plus interest from November 1, 2005 [the day after the agreed Oct. 31, 2005 dissociation date], to the date of judgment. (§ 16701, subd. (b).)**" The Court of Appeal affirmed the trial court's interpretation and application of § 16701(b) (valuing individual assets as liquidated over time and bringing the value "back" to the dissociation date), reversed only the individual liability of the two partners, and corrected a small math error.

**§ 16103 default-rule principle quoted** (at p. 1225): "relations among the partners and between the partners and the partnership are governed by the partnership agreement. To the extent the partnership agreement does not otherwise provide, this chapter governs relations among the partners and between the partners and the partnership." Followed by: "Thus, parties are free to enter into their own agreements concerning the buyout of a dissociating partner's interest. However, in the absence of such an agreement, the buyout of a departing partner's interest in the partnership business is governed by section 16701."

### Han v. Hallberg (2019) 35 Cal.App.5th 621 — **VERIFIED (read operative passages)** — ⚠️ REVIEW GRANTED
CL cluster 6240500, opinion 6107449 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6240500/han-v-hallberg/). Dental partnership; partner transferred his interest to his trust; question whether the trust (or trustee-as-such) is the partner and what his death did.

**Construing § 16601 — note the subdivision numbers as the opinion states them.** Individual partner's death is § 16601, subd. **(7)(A)**; the trust provision is subd. **(8)**:
> "Section 16601 of the UPA states that a partner is dissociated from a partnership 'upon the occurrence of any of the following events.' (Corp. Code, § 16601.) Those include, '[i]n the case of a partner who is an individual,' the partner's death. (Id., subd. (7)(A))."

> "the statute contemplates no termination of partnership status when the trustee of a trust is replaced or dies." (at pp. 632–633.)

> "Whether the partner is 'a trust' or 'is acting as a partner by virtue of being a trustee of a trust,' makes no discernable difference; in both cases, the partner is *not* dissociated from the partnership by the death of the trustee. On the contrary, that partner, whether trust or trustee, is dissociated only by 'distribution of the trust's entire transferable interest in the partnership,' and *not* by 'the substitution of a successor trustee.' (§ 16601, subd. (8).)" (at pp. 635–636.)

The opinion expressly disagrees with *Presta v. Tepper* (2009) 179 Cal.App.4th 909 on the trust-as-partner analysis.

**⚠️ Subsequent history flag:** The California Supreme Court **granted review**: *Han v. Hallberg*, No. **S256659**, Aug. 21, 2019 (CL cluster 8256537; reported at 250 Cal.Rptr.3d 720, 446 P.3d 234). The ultimate disposition of the review proceeding is **not in CourtListener** and was not verified in this pass. **Do not cite Han for any purpose until its current status and citability under Cal. Rules of Court, rule 8.1115(e) are confirmed on Westlaw.**

### Corrales v. Corrales (2011) 198 Cal.App.4th 221 — **VERIFIED (read operative passages)**
CL cluster 5810484, opinion 5665925 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5810484/corrales-v-corrales/). 4th Dist. Div. 3.

Quotes § 16601(1) ("Section 16601, subdivision (1) allows dissociation upon '[t]he partnership's having notice of the partner's express will to withdraw as a partner or on a later date specified by the partner.'") and § 16701(b) verbatim (fn. 5). Holding (at pp. 227–228):
> "A person cannot dissociate from a dissolved partnership, and **the buyout rule of section 16701 does not apply to a two-person partnership when one partner leaves.** When that happens, the dissolution procedures take over. The partnership is wound up, its business is completed, and the partners make whatever adjustments are necessary to their own accounts after paying the creditors. (§ 16807.)"

> "The use of the buyout remedy of section 16701 was error, and we must reverse the judgment and remand it to the trial court with instructions to enter a judicial dissolution of the partnership under sections 16801 and 16807."

**Case-mapping note:** if the Manuel C. Leal & Son Dairy partnership has (or is argued to have) only two partners, *Corrales* threatens the entire § 16701 buyout framework — the remedy would be dissolution/wind-up under §§ 16801/16807, not a § 16701 buyout with interest. Conversely, if the Trust remained a partner via successor trustees (*Han*), there may be no dissociation at all. Both branches must be mapped to our facts.

## PRIORITY 2 — CCP § 998 (memo R1)

### Poster v. Southern California Rapid Transit District (1990) 52 Cal.3d 266 — **VERIFIED (read operative passages + full § 1013 discussion)**
CL cluster/opinion 1442502 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1442502/poster-v-southern-california-rapid-transit-district/). Broussard, J. Issue as framed (at p. 269): "whether when a section 998 offer is served by mail, section 1013, subdivision (a) applies to extend the time to respond by five days."

**Holding (at p. 275):**
> "Accordingly, we conclude that when a statutory settlement offer pursuant to section 998 is served by mail, the provisions of section 1013 apply and extend the 30-day period for acceptance of the offer by 5 days."

Supporting reasoning (at pp. 273–275): § 1013 is "a procedural statute of general application"; "By its terms, section 1013 appears clearly to apply to the time period prescribed by section 998 for accepting statutory offers of compromise"; the 30-day limit is not jurisdictional. Disposition: Court of Appeal **reversed** insofar as it held § 1013 inapplicable; **affirmed** insofar as it held a counteroffer does not revoke a § 998 offer. This resolves R1's flagged confusion: the "§ 1013 does not apply" description in search snippets was the **Court of Appeal's** holding, which the Supreme Court reversed. Post-2005 corroboration: *Licudine* (2019, below) applied the same rule ("§§ 998, subd. (b)(2) [offer expires 30 days after it is made], 1013, subd. (a) [five additional days added for mailed offers]" — *Licudine* at p. 922).

### Gorobets v. Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC (Cal. Aug. 6, 2026, S287946) — **VERIFIED (read holding, background, summary, disposition — chunks 1–2 and 8–9 of 9)**
CL cluster 10940921, opinion 11408504 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10940921/gorobets-v-jaguar-land-rover-north-america-llc/). **The case exists exactly as memo R1 described it**: Supreme Court of California, No. S287946, filed **August 6, 2026**, Corrigan, J., for a unanimous court (Guerrero, C.J., Liu, Kruger, Groban, Evans, JJ., and Feuer, J. [assigned]). Review of *Gorobets v. Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC* (2024) **105 Cal.App.5th 913** (2d Dist. Div. 2, B327745; LA Super. Ct. 19STCV11540). No official Cal.5th cite assigned yet in CL.

**General validity test restated (slip op. p. 1):**
> "Under a well-established test developed by our lower courts, a 998 offer is valid for cost-shifting purposes only if its terms are sufficiently certain or specific to permit the offeree and the trial court to assess its value as of the time the offer was made. (Valentino v. Elliott Sav-On Gas, Inc. (1988) 201 Cal.App.3d 692, 698 …; Fassberg Construction Co. v. Housing Authority of City of Los Angeles (2007) 152 Cal.App.4th 720, 764 …)"

**Holding (slip op. p. 2):**
> "We hold that such a 998 offer can be valid so long as (1) the offer is structured so that it clearly presents the alternatives available to the offeree, and (2) at least one of the two independent sets of terms is sufficiently certain to permit an accurate valuation at the time the offer is made."

**Operative summary (slip op. pp. 30–31):**
> "In this regard, the 998 offer must delineate the specific terms attributable to each choice presented and provide that the offeree must affirmatively choose one or the other set of terms by way of acceptance. … **The rebuffed offeror seeking cost shifting bears the burden of showing its 998 offer was valid, meaning it was sufficiently certain.**"

**Disposition:** "We affirm that portion of the judgment affirming the trial court's award. We reject and overturn its holding that section 998 categorically prohibits alternative-choice offers." Facts match R1 (lump-sum $85,000 alternative vs. itemized-reimbursement alternative; net jury award $76,155.27; cost-shifting upheld because the $85,000 alternative was valid and unbeaten). The Court assumed without deciding that the second (reimbursement/dispute-resolution) alternative was **independently invalid** (fn. 9) — useful: a term structure requiring later itemization, proof, and court determination was treated below as not amenable to valuation, and the Supreme Court did not disturb that.

### M. Lowenstein & Sons, Inc. v. Superior Court (1978) 80 Cal.App.3d 762 — **VERIFIED (read full)** — case is REAL; earlier "possibly nonexistent" flag is resolved
CL cluster/opinion 2144688 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2144688/m-lowenstein-sons-inc-v-superior-court/). Third District (Reynoso, J.; Puglia, P.J., and Paras, J., concurring), filed May 9, 1978; parallel cite 145 Cal.Rptr. 814. The real party in interest was plaintiff **Grant Davidson** — which explains the stray "Lowenstein Sons Inc v. Davidson" name R1's search returned.

**It is a service-of-summons case, not a § 998 case**: out-of-state service by mail on a foreign corporation under CCP § 415.40; constitutionality; and whether a defective return required dismissal under former § 581a. It supports exactly the proposition R1 wanted it for, via the Judicial Council comment it quotes and applies (at p. 770):
> "'Jurisdiction depends on the fact of service, rather than the proof thereof. … Consequently, if the process is in correct form, properly issued and served, and the proof of service is timely filed with the clerk, a defective affidavit will not destroy jurisdiction; the court may permit an amendment of the affidavit to conform to the facts.'"

And its holding (at p. 771): "The trial court could accept the return receipt and the letter as satisfactory evidence of actual delivery to the petitioner. … The authority of a court to allow the filing of a proper affidavit nunc pro tunc to conform to proof is well-established. … We conclude that the return of service, as amended, operated to take the case at bench out of the provisions of section 581a." **Directly useful for defending our misdated proof of service: the fact of service controls, and POS defects are amendable.** (Whether the fact-vs-proof principle carries from summons service to § 998 service is an argument, not a holding — no § 998 case adopting it was located in this pass.)

### Puerta v. Torres (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1267 — **VERIFIED (read full)**
CL cluster 5810288, opinion 5665721 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/5810288/puerta-v-torres/). 4th Dist. Div. 3 (Moore, J.). Quotes § 998(b) verbatim, including that the written offer "shall include a statement of the offer, containing the terms and conditions of the judgment or award, and a provision that allows the accepting party to indicate acceptance of the offer by signing a statement that the offer is accepted" (at pp. 1270–1271). Holding (at p. 1273):
> "This case … deals with the provision that sets forth two mandatory requirements about what *shall* be included in a section 998 offer: the offer shall be written, and it shall contain a provision stating that the recipient can accept the offer 'by signing a statement that the offer is accepted.' The offer at issue here contained nothing regarding acceptance, only the terms of the offer itself and its expiration date. It was therefore invalid under the plain language of the statute, regardless of whether Puerta ever intended to accept the offer or not."

Also describes *Berg v. Darden* (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 721 at length (see cover-letter question below).

### Mostafavi Law Group, APC v. Larry Rabineau, APC (2021) — **VERIFIED (read full)**
CL cluster 4860916, opinion 4664695 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/4860916/mostafavi-law-group-apc-v-larry-rabineau-apc/). 2d Dist. Div. 4 (Currey, J.), B302344, filed 3/3/21, certified for publication. CL lacks the reporter cite; memo cite 61 Cal.App.5th 614 is consistent with the date — confirm on Westlaw. Holding: purported **acceptance** of a § 998 offer lacking an acceptance provision produces a **void judgment**; trial court's vacatur under § 473(d) affirmed. Key language:
> "where a section 998 offer is invalid based on its failure to satisfy all of the 'statutorily required elements[,] … there is nothing for the receiving party to accept' in the first place. (Perez, supra, 206 Cal.App.4th at p. 426.)"

**Footnote 6 — terms must live in the offer itself:**
> "had Rabineau's section 998 offer contained an acceptance provision, and had it been accepted in accordance with that provision, Rabineau could not have later conditioned payment of the settlement funds on MLG's execution of a settlement agreement containing terms not specified in the offer … Rabineau's attempt to introduce additional terms outside of the offer is inconsistent with the plain language of section 998, subdivision (b), which requires the offer itself to 'contain[] the terms and conditions of the judgment or award.'"

### Barella v. Exchange Bank (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 793 — **VERIFIED (read full)**
CL cluster/opinion 2257340 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2257340/barella-v-exchange-bank/). 1st Dist. Div. 5. Review denied Jan. 24, 2001 (noted at end of opinion). Three verified holdings:

1. **Burden on offeror** (at p. 799): "In interpreting section 998, this court has placed squarely on the offering party the burden of demonstrating that the offer is a valid one under section 998. (Taing v. Johnson Scaffolding Co. (1992) 9 Cal.App.4th 579, 585.)"
2. **Strict construction** (at p. 799): "The corollary to this rule is that a section 998 offer must be strictly construed in favor of the party sought to be subjected to its operation. (Garcia v. Hyster Co. (1994) 28 Cal.App.4th 724, 732-733.)" Also: "while the statute contemplates that an offer … may properly include nonmonetary terms and conditions, the offer itself must, nonetheless, be *unconditional*. (Hutchins v. Waters (1975) 51 Cal.App.3d 69, 73.)"
3. **Non-monetary term incapable of valuation invalidates** (at p. 803): "We conclude that the task of valuing a confidentiality clause attached to a settlement offer in a defamation action is too subjective and, therefore, cannot be done. Thus, a confidentiality condition in such a settlement offer will render the offer invalid for purposes of shifting costs to the plaintiff." The opinion adopts *Valentino*'s framework, including that terms may "render it difficult to accurately value the monetary term of the offer so the court cannot fairly determine whether the damage award is 'more favorable'" (quoting *Valentino*, 201 Cal.App.3d at pp. 697–698, at Barella pp. 800–801).

### Licudine v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2019) 30 Cal.App.5th 918 — **VERIFIED (read full)**
CL cluster 6240159, opinion 6107092 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6240159/licudine-v-cedars-sinai-med-ctr/). **2d Dist. Div. 2** (Hoffstadt, J.; Ashmann-Gerst, Acting P.J., Chavez, J.) — CL's "5th District" label is wrong. **Three-factor test (at pp. 924–926):** "(1) how far into the litigation the 998 offer was made; (2) the information available to the offeree prior to the 998 offer's expiration; and (3) whether the offeree let the offeror know it lacked sufficient information to evaluate the offer, and how the offeror responded."

**Burden allocation (at p. 926):**
> "Although the party making a 998 offer generally has the burden of showing that her offer is valid (Timed Out, supra, 21 Cal.App.5th at p. 942; Barella, supra, 84 Cal.App.4th at p. 799), it is the 998 offeree who bears the burden of showing that an otherwise valid 998 offer was not made in good faith. (Elrod, supra, 195 Cal.App.3d at p. 700 …)"

### The cover-letter question (terms in the offer document vs. transmittal letter)
No case squarely holding that a monetary term stated **only in a cover letter** can or cannot supply a § 998 term was located in this pass. What is verified:
- § 998(b) as quoted in *Puerta*/*Mostafavi*: the **written offer** "shall include a statement of the offer, containing the terms and conditions of the judgment or award."
- *Mostafavi* fn. 6 (quoted above): terms outside the offer are inconsistent with § 998(b).
- *Gorobets* (slip op. p. 30): the offer "must delineate the specific terms attributable to each choice presented."
- *Berg v. Darden* (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 721 — **EXISTS–NOT-READ** (described in *Puerta*, which I read): a § 998 offer that was "the last paragraph of a letter addressed to other issues" was held valid; "[a]n otherwise clear section 998 offer is not rendered invalid simply because it does not track precisely the language of the statute" (*Berg* at p. 728, as quoted in *Puerta* at p. 1272). *Berg* cuts toward substance-over-form for the offer's format, but it involved an offer **contained in** the letter, not a term outside the offer document. Read *Berg* directly before using.

## PRIORITY 3 — Civ. Code § 3390 / failed valuation mechanisms

### Bewick v. Mecham (1945) 26 Cal.2d 92 — **VERIFIED (read full)**
CL cluster/opinion 1134157 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/1134157/bewick-v-mecham/). Traynor, J. Lease option to purchase at a price "to be fixed by arbitration" (three appraisers); lessor refused to appoint; trial court **fixed the price itself ($9,000) and decreed specific performance; affirmed.**

- **Appraisal ≠ arbitration** (at pp. 97–98): CCP §§ 1280/1283 (old law) inapplicable because "it is clear that the parties intended not a formal arbitration, but an evaluation by appraisers whom they regarded as experienced and familiar with the conditions in question."
- **Prevention doctrine** (at p. 99): "Each party to a contract has a duty to do everything that the contract presupposes that he will do to accomplish its purpose … and a duty not to prevent or hinder performance by the other party. … A party who prevents fulfillment of a condition of his own obligation commits a breach of contract … and cannot rely on such condition to defeat his liability."
- **Court fixes the price under Civ. Code § 1613** (at pp. 99–100): "Under this section impossibility of ascertaining the price by the contractual method does not preclude a court from giving effect to the contract, ascertaining the consideration in place of the arbitrators or appraisers. Determination of the value of the property is a common task of courts in condemnation, partition, and other proceedings."
- Quoting *Kaufmann v. Liggett* (at p. 100): a court of equity "will in such case make an appraisement itself, or direct it to be done by its own officer, and will thereafter enforce specific performance of the contract upon the terms so found."

### Petrolink, Inc. v. Lantel Enterprises (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 375 — **VERIFIED (read operative passages)**
CL cluster 6239476, opinion 6106390 (https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/6239476/petrolink-inc-v-lantel-enters/); parallel cite 230 Cal.Rptr.3d 283. Panel includes Huffman, Acting P.J., and Guerrero, J. — Fourth District, Division One (CL's "5th District" label is wrong). Lease purchase option at fair market value; dueling party appraisals ($1,615,000 vs. $320,000).

**Mechanism actually used — court-appointed expert under Evidence Code § 730, not § 1281.6 and not an order that a party employ an appraiser:**
> "At trial, both of the appraisers that had been hired by the parties testified, as did an independent appraiser whom the trial court had appointed as a court expert pursuant to Evidence Code section 730."

The trial court adopted a value based on the court expert's appraisal ($789,000 plus "bonus rent," total $889,845) and decreed specific performance. **Disposition on appeal:** "The judgment of the trial court is reversed. The trial court is directed to determine the reasonable date on which the contract for purchase and sale should have been performed, and is further directed to consider what financial adjustments must be made in order to relate the parties' performance back to the date that the contract should have been performed." The valuation methodology itself was left intact; reversal concerned relation-back financial adjustments for the delayed performance. (A 2022 unpublished follow-up appeal exists: CL cluster 6623657.)

### Civ. Code § 3390(b)/(d) applied to compel hiring a specific professional — **NOT-FOUND**; closest authority is adverse-adjacent
No California case was located applying § 3390 (or its subdivisions on personal service / procuring the act of a third person) to an order compelling a party to **employ or retain a named professional**. The closest verified authority points the other way:

**Woolley v. Embassy Suites, Inc. (1991) 227 Cal.App.3d 1520 — VERIFIED (read operative passages)** (CL cluster/opinion 2142292): 
> "it is a fundamental rule that specific performance cannot be decreed to enforce a contract for personal services, regardless of which party seeks enforcement. (Civ. Code, § 3390, subds. (1) & (2); Barndt v. County of Los Angeles (1989) 211 Cal.App.3d 397, 403.)" 

and, quoting Corbin, the rule covers "contracts for the rendition of a performance that is of a distinctly personal and non-delegable character … [including] *managers*, sales agents …" — "The rule is statutory (Civ. Code, § 3390)." (Note: older cases cite subds. (1) & (2); the current lettered subdivisions must be confirmed on Westlaw.) **Drafting implication:** the safer prayer, squarely supported by *Bewick* and *Petrolink*, is for the **court** to determine value (itself, by reference, or via an Evid. Code § 730 expert) — not an order that defendants "employ" Correia-Xavier and Moss Adams.

### CCP § 1281.6 / appraisal-as-arbitration — insurance context verified; non-insurance NOT-FOUND
**Khorsand v. Liberty Mutual Fire Ins. Co. (2018) 20 Cal.App.5th 1028 — VERIFIED (read operative passage)** (CL cluster 6239462, opinion 6106376):
> "Because appraisals are agreement-based arbitration proceedings subject to the statutory scheme regulating nonjudicial arbitration (Code Civ. Proc., § 1280 et seq.), we look to that scheme to establish the meaning of the term 'arbitrator.' The sole statutory regulation within the scheme concerning the selection of arbitrators is Code of Civil Procedure section 1281.6, which provides that courts must enforce the method of selecting arbitrators set forth in the agreement."

That is an **insurance-policy appraisal** case. No published California case was located in this pass treating a **non-insurance appraisal clause** as an arbitration agreement and using § 1281.6 to appoint the appraiser. Note the tension to brief carefully: *Bewick* (1945) held the parties' appraisal provision was **not** arbitration under the then-current statutes, but the modern § 1280(a) definition (quoted in memo R2 from Legislative Counsel text) includes "valuations, appraisals and similar proceedings." *Banwait v. Hernandez* (1988) 205 Cal.App.3d 823 (**EXISTS–NOT-READ**) appeared in the § 1281.6 search results and may address court appointment when the agreed method fails; read before citing.

## (a) CORRECTIONS — what R1–R4 (and the verification tasking) got wrong or can now be fixed

1. **M. Lowenstein & Sons is real — lift R1's "do not cite" flag (¶¶ 189, 287).** 80 Cal.App.3d 762 (3d Dist. 1978) exists; the "Davidson" name in R1's search was the real party in interest. It supports precisely the fact-of-service vs. proof-of-service proposition R1 wanted (quotes and pins above). It is a summons-service case; do not describe it as § 998 authority.
2. **Poster ambiguity resolved (R1 ¶ 36).** The "§ 1013 does not apply" statement was the Court of Appeal's holding; the Supreme Court reversed it. Holding pin: 52 Cal.3d at p. 275 (35-day window for mailed offers). *Licudine* (2019) applies the same rule post-amendments.

Hallberg* opinion, the trust clause ("distribution of the trust's entire transferable interest … but not merely by reason of the substitution of a successor trustee") is § 16601, subd. **(8)**; subd. (7)(A) is death of an individual partner. R4's substance is confirmed verbatim; its subdivision cite "(7)" should be corrected to "(8)" (and re-checked against the current official code, since paragraph numbering is exactly the kind of thing unofficial sources garble).
4. **Han v. Hallberg cannot be cited yet.** Review was granted, S256659 (Aug. 21, 2019; 446 P.3d 234). Final disposition not verifiable in CourtListener. KeyCite before any use.
5. **Gorobets confirmed in full (R1 ¶¶ 38, 121, 211–217, 249, 253, 269).** S287946, filed 8/6/2026, Corrigan, J., unanimous; below: 105 Cal.App.5th 913. R1's "reported" holding language was accurate; the verified opinion adds two load-bearing sentences R1 lacked: the offer "must delineate the specific terms attributable to each choice … and provide that the offeree must affirmatively choose one or the other set of terms by way of acceptance," and "[t]he rebuffed offeror seeking cost shifting bears the burden of showing its 998 offer was valid." R2's structural-analogy framing is right; any draft that cites Petrolink for compelling defendants to employ our named appraisers overreads it. Also note: judgment was reversed/remanded on delayed-performance adjustments (valuation approach intact).
8. **Licudine's court.** 2d Dist. Div. 2 — CL's district metadata (which says 5th) is wrong; same error on Petrolink (actually 4th Dist. Div. 1). Never copy CL court labels into a brief.
9. **"Estate of Kraus" caption.** CL indexes it as "Regents of the University of California v. Kraus"; same case, 184 Cal.App.4th 103. An automated citator name-mismatch warning on this cite is a false positive.
