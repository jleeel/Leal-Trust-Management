## Issue

1. What is the outer boundary of civil discovery into the financial records of an operating partnership under the California Civil Discovery Act, and what proportionality limits apply?
2. What protective-order relief is available against document demands and interrogatories that are overbroad, unduly burdensome, or oppressive?
3. What must a document demand do to be enforceable — i.e., what does "reasonable particularity" require, and what exposure attaches to "any and all documents" demands?
4. How does a partner's, former partner's, or deceased partner's successor's **statutory** right to partnership books and records (Corp. Code §§ 16403–16405) differ from civil discovery, and is that statutory right bounded in time or purpose?
5. What information rights, if any, does a **dissociated partner** or a mere **transferee/successor-in-interest** hold, as against a current partner (Corp. Code §§ 16503, 16601, 16603, 16701)?
6. Can a **trust beneficiary** reach partnership-level books of an entity in which the trust holds an interest, or do the beneficiary's rights run only against the trustee?
7. What grounds exist to resist a **retroactive audit demand reaching back decades** — burden, proportionality, limitations periods, laches, release, and the reality that some records no longer exist?
8. What is the effect of the **December 7, 2017 Settlement Agreement and Mutual Release** on the permissible temporal scope of discovery? Can a party discover into a period whose claims are released?
9. Is **cost-shifting** available for burdensome ESI or for reconstruction of historical accounting records?

## Short Answer

**Scope is broad but expressly bounded.** CCP § 2017.010 permits discovery of any non-privileged matter relevant to the subject matter or reasonably calculated to lead to admissible evidence, and California courts construe that liberally, resolving doubts in favor of discovery (*Greyhound*; *Williams*). But § 2017.020(a) makes limitation **mandatory** ("the court **shall** limit") where burden, expense, or intrusiveness "clearly outweighs" the likelihood the discovery will lead to admissible evidence, and § 2019.030 requires restriction where discovery is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, obtainable from a more convenient/less burdensome/less expensive source, or unduly burdensome or expensive "taking into account the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake."

**Protective orders are the vehicle.** CCP §§ 2031.060(b) and 2030.090 both authorize, for good cause, "any order that justice requires" to protect against "unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense," up to and including an order that categories of items "need not be produced at all."

**Overbreadth is a real and separately stated defect.** CCP § 2031.030(c)(1) requires each demand to describe items with reasonable particularity. *Calcor* confirms discovery is "broad" but "not limitless," and that a demanding party may not shift its own investigative burden onto the responding party. *Obregon* supplies the sharper point: requests "grossly overbroad on their face" and not reasonably related to a legitimate discovery need can support an inference of intent to harass and improperly burden.

**The statutory books-and-records right is a different animal from discovery — and it is time-bounded.** Corp. Code § 16403(b) gives partners access to partnership books, but gives **former** partners access only to "books and records pertaining to the period during which they were partners." Section 16403(c)(2) extends an information right to a partner and to the legal representative of a **deceased** partner, but expressly qualified — "except to the extent the demand or the information demanded is **unreasonable or otherwise improper under the circumstances**." That built-in reasonableness limiter is the single most useful defensive phrase in the partnership statutes for resisting a decades-deep audit demand asserted as a statutory (rather than discovery) right. Section 16405(b) is the enforcement vehicle (an action, not a discovery motion), and § 16405(c) adds a critical temporal principle: "The accrual of, and any time limitation on, a right of action for a remedy under this section is governed by other law. **A right to an accounting upon a dissolution and winding up does not revive a claim barred by law.**"

**A mere transferee has materially lesser rights.** Corp. Code § 16503 provides that a transfer does not entitle the transferee, during the continuance of the partnership, to participate in management, "to require access to information concerning partnership transactions, or to inspect or copy the partnership books or records" — and that on dissolution a transferee is entitled to an account of partnership transactions "**only from the date of the latest account agreed to by all of the partners.**" Whether the co-trustees here are partners, dissociated partners, or transferees is therefore a threshold characterization question that drives the entire information analysis. Note § 16601(7): distribution of a trust's **entire** transferable interest dissociates the trust-partner, "but **not merely by reason of the substitution of a successor trustee**" — so the mere fact that these are *successor* co-trustees does not, standing alone, dissociate the Trust.

**Beneficiary information rights run against the trustee, not the entity.** Probate Code §§ 16060–16063 and 16061 impose reporting and accounting duties on the **trustee**; § 17200 supplies the petition to compel. No retrieved authority establishes a direct beneficiary right to inspect the books of a partnership in which the trust holds an interest, and Corp. Code § 16503 points the other way.

**Retroactive audit demands are resistible — but only on an evidentiary record.** The grounds are proportionality (§§ 2017.020, 2019.030), overbreadth (§ 2031.030), limitations (CCP §§ 343, 338(d); *Manok*; Corp. Code § 16405(c)), release, laches, and non-existence of records (§ 2031.230). The controlling caution is that *West Pico* and *Williams* place the evidentiary burden squarely on the **objecting** party: "oppression must not be equated with burden," and a burden objection "must be sustained by evidence showing the quantum of work required." *Mead Reinsurance* shows what a sufficient showing looks like (≈13,000 files, ≈1,083 man-hours). Boilerplate will not do, and may itself be sanctionable (*Korea Data Systems*).

**On the 2017 release, counsel should expect a two-sided answer.** No retrieved California authority squarely holds that a general release truncates the permissible temporal scope of discovery. The honest analysis is that a release narrows the **claims**, and claim-narrowing narrows **relevance** — but it does not create a hard evidentiary wall, because pre-release facts may remain relevant to (a) the validity/scope of the release itself, (b) unknown claims not waived under Civ. Code § 1542 if the waiver is absent or defective, and (c) opening balances that carry forward into the post-release period. Counsel may consider that our own affirmative discovery ambitions are subject to the identical logic.

**Cost-shifting exists but is narrow.** CCP § 2031.060 (ESI-not-reasonably-accessible provisions) permits the court, on a good-cause showing, to set conditions "including allocation of the expense of discovery"; CCP § 2031.280(e) provides that translation of data compilations into reasonably usable form is done "at the reasonable expense of the **demanding** party"; and *Toshiba* is the leading California cost-allocation decision on legacy backup media. *Williams* separately identifies "a shifting of costs" as an alternative a court should weigh **before** denying discovery outright — meaning cost-shifting can be a compromise the court imposes on us as easily as one it grants us.

### A. General scope and mandatory proportionality limits

**CCP § 2017.010 — Scope of discovery. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: "Unless otherwise limited by order of the court in accordance with this title, any party may obtain discovery regarding any matter, not privileged, that is relevant to the subject matter involved in the pending action or to the determination of any motion made in that action, if the matter either is itself admissible in evidence or appears reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence." Further: "Discovery may relate to the claim or defense of the party seeking discovery or of any other party to the action," and may be had of "the existence, description, nature, custody, condition, and location of any document, electronically stored information, tangible thing, or land or other property."
*Significance:* the enabling provision — and note its opening clause, which subordinates scope to court-ordered limits.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-4/title-4/chapter-2/article-1/section-2017-010/

**CCP § 2017.020(a) — Mandatory limitation. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: "The court **shall** limit the scope of discovery if it determines that the burden, expense, or intrusiveness of that discovery clearly outweighs the likelihood that the information sought will lead to the discovery of admissible evidence." Determination may be made on motion for protective order, accompanied by a § 2016.040 meet-and-confer declaration.
*Significance:* the workhorse proportionality provision for resisting a decades-deep audit. Note "shall," and note the demanding "clearly outweighs" threshold.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-4/title-4/chapter-2/article-1/section-2017-020/

**CCP § 2019.030 — Restricting frequency or extent. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: the court "shall restrict the frequency or extent of use of a discovery method" if it determines either that (1) "the discovery sought is unreasonably cumulative or duplicative, or is obtainable from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive," or (2) "the selected method of discovery is unduly burdensome or expensive, taking into account the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake in the litigation." Available on motion for protective order with a § 2016.040 declaration; monetary sanctions are mandatory against a party who unsuccessfully makes or opposes the motion absent substantial justification.
*Significance:* the "obtainable from some other source" prong is directly applicable where the demanding party seeks reconstruction of records that exist in a more accessible form elsewhere (e.g., bank-produced statements, accountant work files, tax returns).
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2022/code-ccp/part-4/title-4/chapter-5/article-1/section-2019-030/

**Greyhound Corp. v. Superior Court (1961) 56 Cal.2d 355. [S-CONF] — pin cite not confirmed.**
Foundational Discovery Act decision; emphasized liberal construction, and the purpose of doing away with "the sporting theory of litigation — namely, surprise at the trial," making trial "less a game of blindman's buff and more a fair contest with the basic issues and facts disclosed to the fullest practicable extent."
*Caution:* this authority cuts **against** a party resisting discovery. Cite it only where helpful; expect opposing counsel to lead with it.
URL: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2d/56/355.html

**Williams v. Superior Court (2017) 3 Cal.5th 531. [S-CONF] — pin cite not confirmed.**
Text returned: "In the absence of privilege, the right to discovery in this state is a broad one, to be construed liberally so that parties may ascertain the strength of their case and at trial the truth may be determined"; under a "very liberal and flexible standard of relevancy," "any doubts as to relevance should generally be resolved in favor of permitting discovery." On burden allocation: "As the objecting party, Marshalls had the burden of supplying supporting evidence, but in response to Williams's motion to compel it offered none"; an "objection based upon burden must be sustained by evidence showing the quantum of work required." The Court also discussed considering alternatives such as **partial disclosure or a shifting of costs** before settling on complete denial of discovery.
*Significance:* the current Supreme Court statement of both the liberal-scope rule and the objector's evidentiary burden. It is simultaneously our best cost-shifting hook and our biggest obstacle to unsupported burden objections.
URL: https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/williams-v-super-ct-34534

### B. Protective orders

**CCP § 2031.060 — Protective order, documents/ESI. [S-CONF]**
Subd. (a) text returned: the responding party "and any other party or affected person, may promptly move for a protective order," accompanied by a § 2016.040 meet-and-confer declaration.
Subd. (b) text returned: "The court, for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other person from **unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense**," including directions "(1) That all or some of the items or categories of items in the demand need not be produced or made available at all," and (2) that the response time be extended; also that the place of production be changed and that inspection be on specified terms and conditions.
ESI provisions returned (subdivision lettering **not** independently confirmed — see Gaps): the party seeking protection on the basis that ESI is "from a source that is not reasonably accessible because of undue burden or expense" **bears the burden of demonstrating** that fact; if established, "the court may nonetheless order discovery if the demanding party shows good cause"; if the court finds good cause, "the court may set conditions for the discovery of the electronically stored information, **including allocation of the expense of discovery**"; and the court "shall limit the frequency or extent of discovery of electronically stored information, even from a source that is reasonably accessible," if the information is obtainable "from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive."
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-4/title-4/chapter-14/article-1/section-2031-060/

**CCP § 2030.090 — Protective order, interrogatories. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: the court "for good cause shown, may make any order that justice requires to protect any party or other natural person or organization from unwarranted annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression, or undue burden and expense," including that "the set of interrogatories, or particular interrogatories in the set, need not be answered"; that the response time be extended; and that answers be sealed and opened only on court order. Where the ground is that the number of specially prepared interrogatories is unwarranted, "the propounding party shall have the burden of justifying the number." Mandatory monetary sanctions against the unsuccessful movant or opponent absent substantial justification.
URL: https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/code-of-civil-procedure/ccp-sect-2030-090/

**West Pico Furniture Co. v. Superior Court (1961) 56 Cal.2d 407. [S-CONF] — pin cite not confirmed.**
Text returned: "burden" alone is not a statutory ground for objection — the statutes speak of annoyance, expense, embarrassment or oppression, "indicating a legislative acknowledgment that some burden is inherent in all demands for discovery"; "**Oppression must not be equated with burden**"; "the objection based upon burden must be sustained by **evidence showing the quantum of work required**"; and "to support an objection of oppression there must be some showing that the ultimate effect of the burden is **incommensurate with the result sought**."
*Significance:* the standard we must satisfy with declarations, not argument. Facts involved interrogatories requiring a search of records in 78 branch offices; the objecting party's showing was held insufficient.
URL: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/2d/56/407.html

**Mead Reinsurance Co. v. Superior Court (1986) 188 Cal.App.3d 313. [S-CONF] — pin cite not confirmed.**
Order compelled production of "claims files relating to every claim similar to the claim at issue" over a roughly six-and-a-half-year span; responding party would have had to sort approximately 13,000 claims files, estimated at ~1,083.33 man-hours. Court of Appeal held the order oppressive and requiring modification (also on privacy grounds).
*Significance:* the closest retrieved analogue to a volumetric burden showing. It is the template for the declaration a retroactive-audit protective order motion would need.
URL: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/188/313.html

### C. Overbreadth, particularity, and motions to compel further

**CCP § 2031.030(c)(1) — Reasonable particularity. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: each demand in a set shall "Designate the documents, tangible things, land or other property, or electronically stored information to be inspected, copied, tested, or sampled either by specifically describing each individual item or by **reasonably particularizing each category of item**."
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2020/code-ccp/part-4/title-4/chapter-14/article-1/section-2031-030/

**CCP § 2031.310 — Motion to compel further response. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: available where a statement of compliance is incomplete, a representation of inability to comply is "inadequate, incomplete, or evasive," or "an objection in the response is without merit or too general"; "The motion shall set forth **specific facts showing good cause justifying the discovery sought** by the demand"; motion must be accompanied by a § 2016.040 meet-and-confer declaration; and the 45-day rule — "Unless notice of this motion is given within 45 days of the service of the verified response, or any supplemental verified response, or on or before any specific later date to which the demanding party and the responding party have agreed in writing, the demanding party **waives** any right to compel a further response."
*Significance:* two levers. First, the **45-day jurisdictional-style waiver** — calendar it in both directions. Second, "good cause" is the demanding party's burden to carry with specific facts, which is where an unfocused decades-long audit demand is most vulnerable.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-4/title-4/chapter-14/article-2/section-2031-310/

**Calcor Space Facility, Inc. v. Superior Court (1997) 53 Cal.App.4th 216. [S-CONF] — pin cite not confirmed.**
Holding as returned: a records demand must describe the documents to be produced with reasonable particularity; both the nonparty-subpoena provision and the party-inspection provision require records be designated "either by specifically describing each individual item or by reasonably particularizing each category of item." The court observed that "although the scope of civil discovery is **broad, it is not limitless**," and reasoned that a demanding party cannot specifically describe an individual item without first ascertaining its existence — the same requirement generally applying to categories.
*Significance:* the leading California overbreadth/particularity decision, and the standard cite for the proposition that the demanding party may not offload its investigative work onto the responding party.
URL: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/53/216.html

**Obregon v. Superior Court (1998) 67 Cal.App.4th 424. [S-CONF] — pin cite not confirmed.**
Text returned: where discovery requests are "**grossly overbroad on their face** and do not appear reasonably related to a legitimate discovery need, a reasonable inference can be drawn of an intent to harass and improperly burden." The decision also addresses the adequacy of meet-and-confer efforts under the informal-resolution requirement, and cautions that summary denial of discovery for inadequate meet-and-confer "should be reserved for cases of clear intent to burden or harass, cases of clear flaunting of statutory responsibilities, and cases of established track records of lack of good faith."
*Significance:* the best retrieved authority permitting a court to infer improper purpose from facial overbreadth alone — directly usable against a "produce everything since 1994" demand. But the second half also disciplines **our** meet-and-confer conduct.
URL: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/67/424.html

**Korea Data Systems Co. v. Superior Court (1997) 51 Cal.App.4th 1513. [S-CONF] — pin cite not confirmed.**
Text returned: the party filed timely but "boiler plate" objections "lacking the specificity the statute mandates"; the court recognized that use of such boilerplate objections "may be sanctionable," but held that a judicially imposed **waiver of the attorney-client privilege is not an authorized sanction** for failure to serve a timely privilege log.
*Significance:* a two-edged authority. It confirms boilerplate objections carry sanctions exposure (our risk), while capping the sanction available against us (our protection).
URL: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/51/1513.html

**SCC Acquisitions, Inc. v. Superior Court (2015) 243 Cal.App.4th 741. [S-CONF] — pin cite not confirmed.**
Text returned: the right of privacy "protects against the unwarranted, compelled disclosure of private or personal information and extends to one's confidential financial affairs as well as to the details of one's personal life"; **corporations do not hold a California constitutional privacy right**, so a claimed corporate privacy interest is resolved by a **balancing test** weighing relevance and the § 2017.010 standard against the entity's privacy interest; where the **constitutional** right is implicated, the party seeking discovery "must demonstrate a compelling need for discovery, and that compelling need must be so strong as to outweigh the privacy right."
*Significance:* relevant to whether the Partnership (a general partnership, not a corporation) and the individual partners can assert financial-privacy protection over historical books. **[UNVERIFIED]** whether a California general partnership is treated like a corporation (no constitutional right, balancing only) or like a natural person for these purposes — no authority on that specific point was retrieved.
URL: https://www.leagle.com/decision/incaco20151211031

### D. Statutory books-and-records rights (distinct from civil discovery)

**Corp. Code § 16403 — Partnership books and records. [S-CONF]**
Subd. (a) text returned: "A partnership shall keep its books and records, **if any**, in writing or in any other form capable of being converted into clearly legible tangible form, at its chief executive office." *(Some sources render the location as "principal office"; the variance is noted and unresolved — see Gaps.)*
Subd. (b) text returned: "A partnership shall provide partners and their agents and attorneys access to its books and records. It shall provide **former partners** and their agents and attorneys access to books and records **pertaining to the period during which they were partners**. The right of access provides the opportunity to inspect and copy books and records during ordinary business hours. A partnership may impose a **reasonable charge, covering the costs of labor and material, for copies of documents furnished**."
Subd. (c) text returned: "Each partner and the partnership shall furnish to a partner, and to the **legal representative of a deceased partner or partner under legal disability**, both of the following…: (1) Without demand, any information concerning the partnership's business and affairs reasonably required for the proper exercise of the partner's rights and duties under the partnership agreement or this chapter; and (2) On demand, any other information concerning the partnership's business and affairs, **except to the extent the demand or the information demanded is unreasonable or otherwise improper under the circumstances**."
*Significance — three points counsel should note.* (i) The statutory right is exercised against the **partnership**, on-site, during ordinary business hours — it is not a discovery device and does not carry discovery's compulsion machinery. (ii) It is expressly **temporally bounded for former partners**. (iii) Subd. (c)(2) contains its own **reasonableness limiter**, which is the statutory analogue of a proportionality objection and does not require a protective-order motion to invoke. (iv) Subd. (b)'s cost-recovery clause is a limited, non-discovery cost-shifting mechanism.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-corp/title-2/chapter-5/article-4/section-16403/

**Corp. Code § 16404 — General standards of partner's conduct. [S-CONF]**
Subd. (a): the fiduciary duties a partner owes the partnership and other partners are the duties of loyalty and care in subds. (b) and (c). Subd. (b)(1): duty of loyalty includes "To **account** to the partnership and hold as trustee for it any property, profit, or benefit derived by the partner in the conduct and winding up of the partnership business or derived from a use by the partner of partnership property or information, including the appropriation of a partnership opportunity"; (b)(2) refrain from adverse dealing; (b)(3) refrain from competing before dissolution. Subd. (c): duty of care is "limited to refraining from engaging in **grossly negligent or reckless conduct, intentional misconduct, or a knowing violation of law**." Subd. (d): duties and rights are discharged/exercised consistently with the obligation of good faith and fair dealing.
*Significance:* subd. (c)'s gross-negligence floor is materially more forgiving than an ordinary-negligence standard — relevant to any theory that the managing partner's record-keeping was deficient.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-corp/title-2/chapter-5/article-4/section-16404/

**Corp. Code § 16405 — Actions by partnership and partners. [S-CONF]**
Subd. (a): partnership may sue a partner for breach of the agreement or violation of a duty causing harm to the partnership. Subd. (b): a partner may sue the partnership or another partner "for legal or equitable relief, **with or without an accounting** as to partnership business," to enforce rights under the agreement or under the chapter, "including… The partner's rights under Section 16401, 16403, or 16404," and rights on dissociation to have the interest purchased under § 16701 or § 16701.5. Subd. (c): "The **accrual of, and any time limitation on**, a right of action for a remedy under this section **is governed by other law**. A right to an accounting upon a dissolution and winding up **does not revive a claim barred by law**."
*Significance:* § 16405(c) is the strongest retrieved statutory text against an unbounded retroactive accounting/audit theory. It expressly subordinates accounting remedies to the ordinary limitations regime and forecloses using an accounting to resurrect time-barred claims. It also confirms the statutory books-and-records right is enforced by **action**, not by discovery motion.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2022/code-corp/title-2/chapter-5/article-4/section-16405/

### E. Dissociated partners, transferees, and successors-in-interest

**Corp. Code § 16503 — Transferee of partner's transferable interest. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: a transfer of a partner's transferable interest "does not entitle the transferee, during the continuance of the partnership, to participate in the management or conduct of the partnership business, **to require access to information concerning partnership transactions, or to inspect or copy the partnership books or records**." A transferee **does** have the right to receive distributions the transferor would otherwise be entitled to, and on dissolution and winding up the net amount otherwise distributable to the transferor. And: "in a dissolution and winding up, a transferee is entitled to an account of partnership transactions **only from the date of the latest account agreed to by all of the partners**."
*Significance:* the single most powerful retrieved authority on both dimensions of this memo. It denies a transferee any books-and-records right during the partnership's continuance, and — even at dissolution — imposes an express **temporal floor** on the accounting keyed to the last agreed account. If a prior account (or the 2017 settlement's accounting predicate) can be characterized as "the latest account agreed to by all of the partners," this provision supplies a statutory argument for a hard start date.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-corp/title-2/chapter-5/article-5/section-16503/

**Corp. Code § 16601 — Events causing dissociation. [S-CONF]**
Text returned includes, among the enumerated events: express will to withdraw; an event agreed to in the partnership agreement; expulsion under the agreement; unanimous expulsion where continuing with that partner would be unlawful; "A transfer of all or substantially all of that partner's transferable interest in the partnership, other than a transfer for security purposes, or a court order charging the partner's interest, that has not been foreclosed"; judicial determination of incapacity; "In the case of a partner that is a trust or is acting as a partner by virtue of being a trustee of a trust, **distribution of the trust's entire transferable interest in the partnership, but not merely by reason of the substitution of a successor trustee**"; and the parallel provision for an estate/personal representative.
*Significance:* the trust/successor-trustee clause is directly on point to the parties here and cuts **both** ways — substitution of successor co-trustees does not itself dissociate the Trust (preserving its partner-level information rights), but a completed distribution of the Trust's entire transferable interest would.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/2022/code-corp/title-2/chapter-5/article-6/section-16601/

**Corp. Code § 16603 — Effect of dissociation. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: "Upon a partner's dissociation, the partner's right to participate in the management and conduct of the partnership business terminates." The dissociated partner's duty of loyalty under § 16404(b)(1)–(2) and duty of care under § 16404(c) "continue only with regard to **matters arising and events occurring before the partner's dissociation**." Added by Stats. 1996, ch. 1003, § 2, eff. Jan. 1, 1997.
*Note:* the retrieved text did **not** confirm any cross-reference in § 16603 to § 16403(b) governing a dissociated partner's continuing access rights. **[UNVERIFIED]** — counsel must read § 16603 in full; the dissociated partner's information rights are governed principally by § 16403(b)'s "former partners" clause.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-corp/title-2/chapter-5/article-6/section-16603/

**Corp. Code § 16701 — Purchase of dissociated partner's interest. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: if a partner is dissociated without dissolution, "the partnership shall cause the dissociated partner's interest in the partnership to be purchased for a buyout price determined pursuant to subdivision (b)." The buyout price is "the amount that would have been distributable to the dissociating partner under subdivision (b) of Section 16807 if, on the **date of dissociation**, the assets of the partnership were sold at a price equal to the greater of the liquidation value or the value based on a sale of the entire business as a going concern without the dissociated partner and the partnership was wound up as of that date." Damages for wrongful dissociation under § 16602 and "all other amounts owing, whether or not presently due, from the dissociated partner to the partnership, shall be **offset against** the buyout price." If no purchase agreement is reached "within 120 days after a written demand for payment," the partnership shall pay in cash its estimate of the buyout price with accrued interest, reduced by offsets.
*Significance:* the buyout valuation is **as of the date of dissociation**. That anchors the financially relevant period to a valuation date rather than to the entire life of the partnership — a proportionality argument counsel may consider against an audit reaching back decades before any valuation date.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-corp/title-2/chapter-5/article-7/section-16701/

The Law Revision Commission Comment (as reported by secondary sources) states: "The trustee is under a duty to communicate to the beneficiary information that is reasonably necessary to enable the beneficiary to enforce the beneficiary's rights under the trust or redress a breach of trust." **[UNVERIFIED as to the Comment text** — retrieved only through secondary commentary.**]**

### G. Limitations, laches, release, and non-existent records

**CCP § 343 — Four-year catch-all; partner accounting. [S-CONF, via *Manok*]**
As reported: "an action for relief not hereinbefore provided for must be commenced within four years after the cause of action shall have accrued."

**Manok v. Fishman (1973) 31 Cal.App.3d 208. [S-CONF] — pin cite not confirmed.**
Text returned: "It was long the rule that the four-year period of limitation set forth in Code of Civil Procedure, section 343, is applicable to an action between partners for an accounting." On accrual: "partners have no right to bring an action at law against one another until partnership affairs are wound up and the balance due between the members is agreed upon"; before that the only remedy is in equity for an accounting; the court concluded that "the **repudiation** of the alleged fiduciary relationship of the parties by the defendants was the time when plaintiffs' cause of action for an accounting accrued," and that a partner's right to recover a share of partnership assets accrues "at the time the partner severs his/her ties with the partnership."
*Significance:* accrual, not the transaction date, drives the limitations analysis — which is precisely why an opponent will argue that no pre-2017 transaction is time-barred until repudiation or severance. This authority is genuinely two-sided.
URL: https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/31/208.html

**CCP § 338(d) — Three years, fraud/mistake, delayed accrual. [S-CONF]**
As reported: a fraud claim "does not accrue until the discovery, by the aggrieved party, of the facts constituting the fraud." Secondary sources note authority applying § 338(d)'s three-year period to breaches characterizable as **constructive fraud**. **[UNVERIFIED as to the constructive-fraud line** — no case retrieved.**]**

**Civ. Code § 1542 — General release; unknown claims. [S-CONF]**
Text returned (post-2019 amendment): "A general release does not extend to claims that the **creditor or releasing party** does not know or suspect to exist in his or her favor at the time of executing the release and that, if known by him or her, **would have** materially affected his or her settlement with the debtor or released party."
*Significance:* the December 7, 2017 Settlement Agreement predates the January 1, 2019 amendment, so the **pre-2019** statutory text applies to its construction, and any § 1542 waiver clause should be read against the version then in force. Whether the 2017 agreement contains an express § 1542 waiver is a document question, not a research question, and is the pivot of the entire release analysis.
URL: https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-civ/division-3/part-1/title-4/chapter-6/section-1542/

**Effect of a release on the permissible temporal scope of discovery — [UNVERIFIED].**
**No California authority was located holding that a general release bars, or fails to bar, discovery into the released period.** Targeted searching produced only general relevance commentary. This is a genuine gap, not an omission for brevity. The analysis in this memo proceeds from first principles under §§ 2017.010, 2017.020 and 2019.030 and is expressly labeled as inference, not authority.

**Laches — [UNVERIFIED].**
The commonly stated formulation — (1) unreasonable or inexcusable delay plus (2) prejudice to the defendant, with prejudice recognized in evidentiary form (lost, stale, or degraded evidence; faded memories; deceased witnesses) — was returned **only from law-firm and commentary sources**. **No California case was retrieved.** Counsel must supply verified California authority. Note also that laches is generally a defense to **equitable** relief, which matters because an accounting is an equitable remedy — a point that makes laches unusually well-suited to a retroactive-accounting demand, if it can be supported by verified authority.

**CCP § 2031.230 — Representation of inability to comply. [S-CONF]**
Text returned: "A representation of inability to comply with the particular demand for inspection, copying, testing, or sampling **shall affirm that a diligent search and a reasonable inquiry has been made** in an effort to comply with that demand. This statement shall also specify whether the inability to comply is because the particular item or category **has never existed, has been destroyed, has been lost, misplaced, or stolen, or has never been, or is no longer, in the possession, custody, or control** of the responding party. The statement shall set forth the **name and address of any natural person or organization known or believed** by that party to have possession, custody, or control of that item or category of item."
*Significance:* this is the governing provision for the 1994–1996 ledger gap and the pre-2017 Citizens Business Bank statement gap. It is **not** a shield — it is an affirmative, verifiable, three-part disclosure obligation, and a deficient § 2031.230 statement is an express ground for a motion to compel further under § 2031.310 ("a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive").

## Application to Our Facts

*Framing note: everything in this section is offered as considerations for counsel. Nothing here predicts how any court, and specifically no judge of the Tulare County Superior Court, will rule on any issue.*

### 1. The threshold characterization question drives almost everything

Counsel may consider that the most consequential early determination is **what the successor co-trustees legally are** with respect to the Manuel C. Leal & Son Dairy Partnership:

- **A current partner** (the Trust holds the partnership interest and has not dissociated) — then Corp. Code § 16403(b)–(c) applies at full strength, and the information right is broad, though still subject to § 16403(c)(2)'s "unreasonable or otherwise improper under the circumstances" limiter.
- **A former/dissociated partner** — then § 16403(b)'s "period during which they were partners" clause supplies an express temporal ceiling on statutory access, and § 16603 confirms management rights have terminated.
- **A mere transferee/successor-in-interest** — then § 16503 denies any right "to require access to information concerning partnership transactions, or to inspect or copy the partnership books or records" during the continuance of the partnership, and caps any dissolution accounting at "the date of the latest account agreed to by all of the partners."

Counsel may consider that § 16601(7) is directly relevant and cuts against a reflexive characterization: substitution of **successor** trustees does **not** by itself dissociate a trust-partner. Counsel may therefore wish to determine, from the partnership agreement and the Trust instrument, (a) whether Hazel J. Leal or her Trust was the partner of record, (b) whether any distribution of the Trust's entire transferable interest has occurred, and (c) whether Hazel's death made the co-trustees "the legal representative of a deceased partner" under § 16403(c) — which would confer an information right notwithstanding transferee status.

Counsel may also consider that this characterization is **not** a discovery question and is unlikely to be resolved by a discovery motion. It may be better suited to an early dispositive or declaratory vehicle, with the discovery scope argument built on top of the resulting determination.

### 2. Separating the two tracks: statutory inspection versus civil discovery

Counsel may consider that the co-trustees' demands should be sorted into two piles, because the governing standards differ materially:

| | **Statutory inspection (Corp. Code § 16403)** | **Civil discovery (CCP)** |
|---|---|---|
| Object | The **partnership** | A **party** to this action |
| Standard | Access to books/records; information "except to the extent… unreasonable or otherwise improper" | Relevance + reasonably calculated; capped by §§ 2017.020, 2019.030 |
| Temporal limit | Express for former partners (§ 16403(b)); express floor for transferees (§ 16503) | No express limit; controlled by relevance and proportionality |
| Enforcement | An **action** under § 16405(b) | Motion to compel under § 2031.310 |
| Cost | Partnership may charge reasonable labor/material copying costs (§ 16403(b)) | § 2031.280(e); § 2031.060 ESI allocation |
| Mechanism | On-site inspection during ordinary business hours | Production in response to a demand |

Counsel may consider that conflating the tracks favors the demanding party, and that insisting on the distinction is worth doing early — in particular, that a **§ 16403 inspection right does not entitle its holder to a constructed audit, a reconciled ledger, or a reconstruction of records; it entitles the holder to inspect and copy books and records that exist**, at the chief executive office, during business hours, at the holder's own copying cost. Section 16403(a)'s "**if any**" qualifier is worth noting: the statute contemplates that a partnership may not have books for a given period.

### 3. The retroactive audit demand — grounds counsel may consider

Counsel may consider building any protective-order motion under CCP §§ 2031.060(b) and 2017.020(a) on a stacked showing:

**(a) Proportionality.** Section 2017.020(a) is mandatory ("shall limit") where burden clearly outweighs likely evidentiary yield, and § 2019.030 adds the "needs of the case, the amount in controversy, and the importance of the issues at stake" calculus plus the "obtainable from some other source that is more convenient, less burdensome, or less expensive" prong. Counsel may consider that the second prong is unusually strong here: bank statements are obtainable from Citizens Business Bank by subpoena; tax returns and depreciation schedules are obtainable from the partnership's accountants; K-1s are in the trustees' own predecessor's records. A demand that the **partnership** reconstruct 1994–1996 from scratch, when third-party sources exist, is the paradigm § 2019.030 target.

**(b) Overbreadth and particularity.** Counsel may consider testing each demand against § 2031.030(c)(1) and *Calcor*. If demands are cast as "any and all documents relating to" partnership finances since inception, *Calcor*'s observation that discovery is "broad, but not limitless" and *Obregon*'s inference-of-harassment reasoning are directly available. Counsel may consider that this objection is strongest when made **category by category** with specifics, not as a global refrain.

**(c) Limitations as a relevance constraint.** Counsel may consider that Corp. Code § 16405(c) is the most useful retrieved text: an accounting right "does not revive a claim barred by law," and accrual/limitations are "governed by other law." Coupled with CCP § 343's four-year period for partner accountings (*Manok*) and § 338(d)'s three-year fraud period, counsel may consider arguing that discovery into a period for which **no viable claim can exist** yields nothing "reasonably calculated to lead to the discovery of admissible evidence" under § 2017.010, and therefore fails § 2017.020(a)'s balance.
> **Honest counterweight.** *Manok* holds accrual for a partner accounting runs from **repudiation** of the fiduciary relationship or severance of ties — not from the transaction date. Opposing counsel will argue that nothing accrued until a recent repudiation, so **nothing** is time-barred and the full history is live. Counsel may consider that this is the single strongest response to our limitations argument, and that the argument's viability depends heavily on identifying a repudiation date, which the 2017 Settlement Agreement may or may not supply.

**(d) Laches.** Counsel may consider that an accounting is equitable relief and therefore squarely within laches territory, and that the evidentiary-prejudice branch maps precisely onto our facts (missing 1994–1996 ledgers, a pre-2017 bank statement gap, potential decedent and faded-memory witnesses). **However, no verified California laches authority was retrieved**, and counsel must supply it before this argument is briefed.

**(e) Non-existence of records.** Counsel may consider that CCP § 2031.230 governs and that it is a **duty**, not a defense. See § 5 below — this is an exposure item, not an asset.

### 4. The December 7, 2017 Settlement Agreement and Mutual Release — a genuinely two-sided question

Counsel may consider the release from both directions, because no retrieved authority resolves it.

**The argument that the release narrows discovery:** a release extinguishes the released claims; relevance under § 2017.010 is measured against "the subject matter involved in the pending action"; if pre-December 7, 2017 conduct can give rise to no claim in this action, discovery aimed exclusively at that conduct is not reasonably calculated to lead to admissible evidence, and § 2017.020(a) requires limitation where the burden of that discovery clearly outweighs its yield. On this view December 7, 2017 functions as a presumptive discovery cutoff, subject to exceptions.

**The arguments that it does not — which counsel should assume will be made:**

1. **Validity and scope of the release are themselves in issue.** If the trustees contend the release was procured by concealment or non-disclosure by a fiduciary managing partner, then pre-release conduct is relevant to the release's own enforceability. A release cannot bootstrap itself into immunity from discovery about how it was obtained.
2. **Civ. Code § 1542.** If the 2017 agreement lacks an express § 1542 waiver — or if the waiver is defective — unknown claims are not released, and discovery into the pre-release period may be framed as necessary to identify them. Counsel may consider that this makes the presence, wording, and knowingness of the § 1542 waiver in the December 7, 2017 agreement a **document-review priority**, and note that the pre-2019 version of § 1542 governs.
3. **Carry-forward balances.** Partnership capital accounts are cumulative. If a post-release capital account balance is disputed, its **opening** balance is a function of pre-release activity. Counsel may consider that this is an inherently strong relevance argument for a limited pre-release look-back — arguably the most likely basis on which a court would allow some pre-2017 discovery — and that it is materially narrower than a full audit. Counsel may consider proposing this narrowing affirmatively rather than resisting it wholesale: e.g., production of year-end capital account statements and reconciliations for a defined pre-release period sufficient to establish opening balances, rather than transaction-level records back to 1994.
4. **Background, pattern, and intent.** Pre-release conduct is commonly sought as evidence of a course of dealing bearing on post-release conduct. Counsel may consider that this is a weaker but frequently accepted relevance theory, and that it is best met with proportionality (§ 2017.020(a)) rather than a flat relevance objection.

**Our own exposure on this point.** Counsel may consider that if we argue December 7, 2017 is a hard discovery cutoff, we will find it difficult to seek pre-2017 discovery **from the trustees** — for example, into the predecessor trustee's conduct, into pre-2017 distributions or transfers, or into what Hazel J. Leal knew and when. Counsel may wish to decide early, as a strategic matter, whether the cutoff argument is worth the reciprocal cost, and to consider whether a **mutual, stipulated temporal scope** with defined carve-outs (release validity; opening balances; specifically identified transactions) better serves the client than an asymmetric position that is unlikely to survive contact with a reciprocity argument.

### 5. Our exposure as the partnership's record-keeper — candid assessment

Counsel may consider that our client's position as **managing partner and custodian of the books** inverts several of the advantages above.

- **The burden objection requires evidence, from us.** *West Pico* ("oppression must not be equated with burden"; burden objection "must be sustained by evidence showing the quantum of work required") and *Williams* (objecting party "had the burden of supplying supporting evidence… it offered none") mean that any burden-based resistance requires a **declaration quantifying hours, personnel, and dollars** — ideally from a CPA or forensic accountant, in the *Mead Reinsurance* mold (≈13,000 files / ≈1,083 man-hours). Counsel may consider that assertions about the difficulty of a 30-year audit, unsupported by such a declaration, are unlikely to carry the motion and may expose us to mandatory sanctions under §§ 2031.060 and 2030.090.
- **Boilerplate objections are affirmatively risky.** *Korea Data Systems* recognizes that boilerplate objections "may be sanctionable." Counsel may consider that our responses should be individually reasoned per demand.
- **§ 2031.230 is a disclosure obligation, not a shield.** For the **1994–1996 ledgers** and the **pre-2017 Citizens Business Bank statement gap**, a bare "not in our possession" is expressly identified in § 2031.310 as a ground for a motion to compel further ("a representation of inability to comply is inadequate, incomplete, or evasive"). Counsel may consider that a compliant response must (i) affirm a **diligent search and reasonable inquiry**, (ii) specify **which** of the statutory reasons applies (never existed / destroyed / lost / misplaced / stolen / never or no longer in our possession, custody, or control), and (iii) **name and give the address of** any person or organization believed to have the records — which here plausibly means prior accountants, prior bookkeepers, Citizens Business Bank, and prior counsel. Counsel may consider that documenting the search **contemporaneously** — who looked, where, when, what was found — is prudent, both to satisfy § 2031.230 and to blunt any spoliation or adverse-inference narrative. Counsel may further consider that Corp. Code § 16403(a)'s "if any" qualifier supports the proposition that the statute does not itself impose a perpetual retention duty, though **no authority on partnership record-retention periods was located or verified** and this should not be asserted without one.
- **Duty of care standard may help.** Corp. Code § 16404(c) limits a partner's duty of care to "grossly negligent or reckless conduct, intentional misconduct, or a knowing violation of law." Counsel may consider that record-keeping imperfection over three decades is meaningfully distinct from gross negligence, and that framing the standard early is worthwhile. Counsel should note the countervailing § 16404(b)(1) duty **to account**, and § 16404(d)'s good-faith-and-fair-dealing overlay.
- **Privacy is a weak reed here.** *SCC Acquisitions* indicates corporations lack a constitutional privacy right and are subject only to a balancing test. Whether a general partnership is treated similarly is **unverified**. Counsel may consider that individual partners' personal financial privacy is a stronger and more targeted objection than any entity-level privacy claim, and that it applies only to genuinely personal financial material commingled in partnership files.

### 6. The legacy QuickBooks .QBX file — the sharpest ESI question

Counsel may consider that the 2009/2014 QuickBooks **accountant-transfer (.QBX)** file is the cleanest candidate in this matter for the "**source that is not reasonably accessible because of undue burden or expense**" framework under CCP § 2031.060:

- The **burden of demonstrating** inaccessibility is expressly ours. Counsel may consider that this requires a technical declaration addressing: whether the .QBX format can be opened at all without the originating accountant's QuickBooks version and accountant's copy workflow; whether a license or legacy software must be obtained; whether the file is a full company file or a restricted accountant's-copy transfer file with a dividing date; whether conversion is one-way and destructive; the vendor's version-support horizon; and the estimated cost and hours to extract, convert, validate, and produce.
- If we make that showing, the demanding party must show **good cause**, and if it does, "the court may set conditions… including **allocation of the expense of discovery**." Counsel may consider that this is our most concrete cost-shifting opportunity in the case.
- CCP § 2031.280(e) may independently apply: translating a data compilation "into reasonably usable form" is to be done "at the reasonable expense of the **demanding** party." Counsel may consider that a .QBX file is a strong candidate for a "data compilation" requiring "detection devices" to translate. *Toshiba* (backup tapes; $1.5–1.9M) supplies the analogous cost-allocation precedent, subject to the caveat that it construed the former statute.
- **Two cautions.** First, § 2031.060's "shall limit… even from a source that is reasonably accessible" prong cuts against us if the same data is obtainable from the accountants' current files, from tax returns, or from the post-2014 QuickBooks company file — counsel may consider that we should establish what is **not** duplicative before leaning on this argument. Second, *Williams* identifies cost-shifting as an alternative a court weighs **before denying discovery** — meaning a court inclined to compel may simply order production with a cost split rather than deny it, so counsel may consider whether we would accept that outcome.

### 7. Our affirmative discovery against the trustees — the mirror image

Counsel may consider that we occupy the beneficiary/claimant posture on other issues, and that the same authorities constrain us:

- Prob. Code §§ 16060, 16061, 16062, 16063 and § 17200 supply the framework for obtaining trust-level information and accountings **from the trustees**. Section 16061's "reasonable request" and "relevant to the beneficiary's interest" qualifiers, and § 17200's confinement to the "internal affairs of the trust," are limits on us as well.
- If we seek pre-2017 information from the trustees — predecessor trustee conduct, distributions, transfers — we will encounter our own release-cutoff argument, our own proportionality arguments, and our own "records no longer exist" responses. Counsel may consider that any protective-order position we take should be stress-tested for reciprocity before it is filed.
- Counsel may consider that our demands should be drafted to survive *Calcor* and § 2031.030(c)(1) — categories reasonably particularized, bounded in time, tied to identified claims — both because that is what the statute requires and because it forecloses a tu-quoque response.
- Counsel may consider the **45-day** § 2031.310 waiver period as a calendaring priority in both directions.

3. **Consequence:** no statute and no opinion in this memo was retrieved and read in its primary source. All quoted text came through a web-search index. **No authority in this memo is tagged VERIFIED, and none should be treated as verified.**
4. **Search-index synthesis risk.** Search results are machine-summarized. In at least one instance a summary **contradicted the statutory text it quoted** (CCP § 2031.280(e): the summary said the *responding* party bears the cost; the quoted text says "at the reasonable expense of the **demanding** party"). This is direct evidence that the summaries are not reliable on their own, and it is the reason every proposition here is tagged and every quotation is attributed to quoted text rather than to summary.
5. **Prompt-echo risk.** Some searches supplied a subdivision letter or a proposition in the query itself. Where the response may have echoed the query's framing rather than independently confirming it, this has been flagged in place — specifically for **CCP § 2031.060 subdivisions (d)/(e)/(f)**.
