## Issue

1. What must a written offer contain, and how must it be served, to be a valid offer to compromise under Code of Civil Procedure § 998?
2. What is the effect of defects in the **proof of service** — specifically a POS reciting service and execution "On January 3, 2018," more than eight years before the offer was actually served — on (a) the offer's enforceability and (b) § 998 cost-shifting?
3. Do California courts apply **strict compliance** or **substantial compliance** to § 998? What are the leading authorities on each side?
4. Does an **undated** offer affect validity, or the running of the acceptance period?
5. When does the **30-day acceptance window** under § 998(b)(2) begin, and does CCP § 1013's mail extension enlarge it?
6. Can an offer containing **no monetary term** — here, specific performance requiring defendants to employ two named appraisers — trigger § 998 cost-shifting at all? How is "more favorable judgment" measured against a non-monetary offer?
7. Who bears the **burden** of showing the offer was valid, and separately, that it was reasonable and made in good faith?

## Short Answer

**Threshold correction counsel should note at the outset.** The tasking framed the exposure under § 998(c)(1). Subdivision (c)(1) governs an offer **made by a defendant**. Because our client is the **plaintiff** and made this offer, the operative cost-shifting provision is **§ 998(d)**, which gives the court *discretion* ("may require") to award the plaintiff post-offer expert-witness fees if the defendant fails to obtain a more favorable judgment. The upside is therefore narrower and wholly discretionary. Counsel should also note that Civil Code § 3291 prejudgment interest — the other classic reward for a plaintiff's rejected 998 offer — is by its terms confined to actions "to recover damages for personal injury," and so appears unavailable in a trust/real-property dispute.

**On the merits.** § 998(b)(1) requires that the offer be (i) in writing, (ii) *served* on the other party, (iii) not less than 10 days before trial or arbitration, (iv) containing a statement of the offer with the terms and conditions of the judgment, and (v) containing an acceptance provision. Service is a statutory element of the offer, not a formality collateral to it. California appellate authority on § 998 form requirements is predominantly **strict-compliance and bright-line** (Puerta, Mostafavi, Sanford), and the offer is **construed against its proponent** with validity reviewed **de novo** (Prince). A meaningful counter-current exists — courts have upheld offers against hyper-technical attacks and have allowed contemporaneous writings to resolve ambiguity (Prince, Berg, Goodstein) — but no located authority extends "substantial compliance" to excuse a *missing statutory element*.

The **acceptance window** runs 30 days from when the offer is "made," which practitioner authority uniformly treats as the date of **service**, not the date printed on the document; and secondary sources uniformly report that **Poster v. Southern Cal. Rapid Transit Dist.** (1990) 52 Cal.3d 266 holds § 1013's five-day mail extension **does** apply, giving a mailed offer a 35-day window. Counsel should be aware that this point is **not as clean as it looks in the secondary literature** — search results also surfaced a description of the *Court of Appeal* in Poster holding the opposite before review. That conflict is unresolved on this record and must be settled by reading the opinion.

The **non-monetary term is the largest single vulnerability.** Non-monetary terms do not automatically invalidate a 998 offer, but they must be "sufficiently certain and capable of valuation" so a court can decide whether the judgment beat the offer (Barella). The California Supreme Court reaffirmed a certainty-and-accurate-valuation requirement weeks ago in **Gorobets v. Jaguar Land Rover N. Am., LLC** (Aug. 6, 2026). An offer whose economic content is "value as determined by two appraisers, with discounts as determined by those appraisers" supplies no number ascertainable *at the time the offer was made*; counsel should treat this as the defect most likely to defeat cost-shifting outright.

The **POS defects** cut two ways. Because the offer itself is undated, the POS is the only intrinsic evidence of when the offer was "made" — and it says January 3, 2018, which on its face would place the offer's deemed-withdrawal date in early 2018. Against that, the fact of service and the *proof* of service are analytically distinct, and an obviously impossible date is a strong candidate for scrivener's error curable by extrinsic proof of actual service on 7/9/2026. The missing street address in the envelope address block is a separate, independent defect under CCP § 1013a(3) and § 1013(a).

**Burden.** Licudine allocates it in two parts: the **offeror** bears the burden of showing the offer was **valid**; the **offeree** bears the burden of showing an otherwise valid offer was **not made in good faith**. As offeror, our side carries the burden on every validity defect catalogued below.

### A. Statutes

**Code of Civil Procedure § 998** — the governing statute.
- **(a):** "The costs allowed under Sections 1031 and 1032 shall be withheld or augmented as provided in this section."
- **(b)(1):** "Not less than 10 days prior to commencement of trial or arbitration … any party may serve an offer in writing upon any other party to the action to allow judgment to be taken or an award to be entered in accordance with the terms and conditions stated at that time. The written offer shall include a statement of the offer, containing the terms and conditions of the judgment or award, **and a provision that allows the accepting party to indicate acceptance of the offer by signing a statement that the offer is accepted.**" Acceptance must be in writing and signed by counsel for the accepting party (or the party if unrepresented).
- **(b)(2):** "If the offer is not accepted prior to trial or arbitration or **within 30 days after it is made**, whichever occurs first, it shall be deemed withdrawn, and cannot be given in evidence upon the trial or arbitration."
- **(b)(3):** defines when trial/arbitration is "actually commenced" (opening statement, or oath to first witness, or introduction of evidence).
- **(c)(1):** "If an offer made by **a defendant** is not accepted and the plaintiff fails to obtain a more favorable judgment or award, the plaintiff shall not recover his or her postoffer costs and shall pay the defendant's costs from the time of the offer."
- **(c)(2)(A):** "In determining whether the plaintiff obtains a more favorable judgment, the court or arbitrator shall exclude the postoffer costs."
- **(d):** "If an offer made by **a plaintiff** is not accepted and the defendant fails to obtain a more favorable judgment or award … the court or arbitrator, **in its discretion, may** require the defendant to pay a reasonable sum to cover postoffer costs of the services of expert witnesses, who are not regular employees of any party, actually incurred and reasonably necessary in either, or both, preparation for trial or arbitration, or during trial or arbitration, of the case by the plaintiff, in addition to plaintiff's costs."
- **(e):** where a defendant's offer is not accepted and plaintiff fails to obtain a more favorable judgment, § 998 costs from the time of the offer are deducted from damages; if costs exceed damages, the net is awarded to defendant.
- **Tag:** [TEXT-RETRIEVED-VIA-INDEX]. **URLs indexed:** `https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-2/title-14/chapter-3/section-998/`; `https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/code-of-civil-procedure/ccp-sect-998/`; `https://law.onecle.com/california/civil-procedure/998.html`. **leginfo.legislature.ca.gov was blocked and could not be used.** Subdivisions (f) through (i) were not retrieved and are not summarized here.

**Code of Civil Procedure § 1013(a)** — service by mail; extension of time.
- Requires deposit in a USPS facility, "in a sealed envelope, with postage paid, **addressed to the person on whom it is to be served, at the office address as last given by that person on any document filed in the cause** and served on the party making service by mail; otherwise at that party's place of residence."
- "Service is complete at the time of the deposit, but **any period of notice and any right or duty to do any act or make any response within any period or on a date certain after service** of the document, which time period or date is prescribed by statute or rule of court, **shall be extended five calendar days** upon service by mail, if the place of address and the place of mailing is within the State of California, 10 calendar days if either is outside the State."
- **Tag:** [TEXT-RETRIEVED-VIA-INDEX]. **URLs indexed:** `https://law.justia.com/codes/california/code-ccp/part-2/title-14/chapter-5/section-1013/`; `https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/code-of-civil-procedure/ccp-sect-1013/`.

**Code of Civil Procedure § 1013a** — contents of the proof of service by mail. **This is the provision the POS defects implicate directly.**
- The standard affidavit must show the exact title of the document served, the name and residence or business address of the person making service, that the person is a resident of or employed in the county where the mailing occurs, is over 18 and not a party, "**the date and place of deposit in the mail, the name and address of the person served as shown on the envelope**," and that the envelope was sealed and deposited with postage prepaid.
- The **business-practice alternative** (the box checked on our POS) requires, among other elements: "(D) that he or she is readily familiar with the business practice for collection and processing of correspondence for mailing with the United States Postal Service, (E) that the correspondence would be deposited with the United States Postal Service that same day in the ordinary course of business, **(F) the name and address of the person served as shown on the envelope, and the date and place of business where the correspondence was placed for deposit in the United States Postal Service**, and (G) that the envelope was sealed and placed for collection and mailing on that date following ordinary business practices."

**Code of Civil Procedure § 1015** — where a party has an attorney in the action, service of papers "must be upon the attorney instead of the party," except subpoenas, writs, other process, and papers to bring a party into contempt.
- **Tag:** [TEXT-RETRIEVED-VIA-INDEX]. **URL indexed:** `https://codes.findlaw.com/ca/code-of-civil-procedure/ccp-sect-1015/`.

**Civil Code § 3291** — prejudgment interest at 10% from the date of a plaintiff's first § 998 offer where the defendant does not accept and the plaintiff obtains a more favorable judgment. **By its terms limited to "any action brought to recover damages for personal injury"**; inapplicable to public entities and public employees acting in the scope of employment.

### B. Strict compliance / bright-line authority

**Puerta v. Torres (2011) 195 Cal.App.4th 1267.** Reported to hold that § 998(b) requires the written offer to contain an **acceptance provision**; an offer lacking one is **invalid**, and the offeree's failure to accept does not trigger cost-shifting. Reported reasoning: § 998's language and structure set out **mandatory** requirements; a bright-line rule adds "consistency and predictability to section 998's operation"; where an offer fails to satisfy all statutorily required elements, "there is nothing for the receiving party to accept" in the first place; general contract principles do not override the statutory requirements.
- **Pin cite not confirmed.**
- **Tag:** [CITE CORROBORATED / HOLDING SUMMARIZED]. **URLs indexed:** `https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/1568815.html`; `https://calawyers.org/solo-small-firm/an-offer-you-cant-accept/`; `https://www.hbblaw.com/judgment-stemming-from-a-section-998-offer-without-a-written-acceptance-provision-is-void/`.

**Mostafavi Law Group, APC v. Larry Rabineau, APC (2021) 61 Cal.App.5th 614.** Reported to hold that a § 998 offer lacking an acceptance provision is invalid, and a **judgment entered on the purported acceptance of such an offer is void** and subject to being set aside under CCP § 473(d) — even where the offeree hand-wrote an acceptance on the offer itself and filed a notice of acceptance.
- **Pin cite not confirmed.**
- **Tag:** [CITE CORROBORATED / HOLDING SUMMARIZED]. **URLs indexed:** `https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/2114151.html`; `https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2021/b302344.html`.
- **Significance here:** this is the strongest available demonstration that a § 998 form defect is *not* excused by the parties' evident intent or by actual performance of the acceptance ritual.

**Sanford v. Rasnick (2016) 246 Cal.App.4th 1121.** Reported to hold a joint § 998 offer invalid where it conditioned acceptance on "notarized execution and transmittal of a written settlement agreement and general release" whose terms were never set forth in the offer or communicated while the offer was open. Reported reasoning: the condition made the offer **too indefinite**; a "release" is not the same as a "settlement agreement," whose terms "can require considerable negotiation." Order awarding costs reversed.
- **Pin cite not confirmed.**
- **Tag:** [CITE CORROBORATED / HOLDING SUMMARIZED]. **URLs indexed:** `https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2016/a145704.html`; `https://caselaw.findlaw.com/ca-court-of-appeal/1732998.html`.
- **Significance here:** directly analogous in structure — an offer whose operative economic content is deferred to a future process outside the four corners of the offer.

### C. Valuation / non-monetary and specific-performance offers

**Barella v. Exchange Bank (2000) 84 Cal.App.4th 793.** The central authority for Issue 6. Reported holdings:
- "The inclusion of nonmonetary terms and conditions does not render a Section 998 offer invalid; **but those terms or conditions must be sufficiently certain and capable of valuation to allow the court to determine whether judgment is more favorable than the offer.**"
- Where an offer includes a non-monetary component, the offer must be evaluated "in light of all the terms and conditions attached to that offer and not simply the monetary amount."
- On the facts, a confidentiality condition failed: its "value … is so highly subjective and elusive that no court can determine its monetary worth."
- Also reported for: "the offering party has the burden of demonstrating that the offer is valid under section 998," and that a § 998 offer "must be strictly construed in favor of the party sought to be subjected to its operation."
- **Pin cite not confirmed.** (Secondary sources cite the valuation holding to roughly pp. 799–801; that range is *reported*, not confirmed, and should not be used as a pin cite without reading the opinion.)
- **Tag:** [CITE CORROBORATED / HOLDING SUMMARIZED]. **URLs indexed:** `https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/84/793.html`; `https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/1402552.html`; `https://underwood.law/blog/equity-as-a-settlement-offer-code-of-civil-procedure-998/`.

**Gorobets v. Jaguar Land Rover North America, LLC**, Cal. Supreme Court No. S287946, filed **August 6, 2026**. *Official reporter citation not yet confirmed.* Reported holding: a § 998 offer **may present a choice between alternative sets of terms**, so long as the offer **clearly presents the alternatives** and **at least one alternative is sufficiently certain to permit an accurate valuation at the time the offer is made**; if the judgment does not exceed the highest-valued valid alternative, cost-shifting is permitted. Facts: Land Rover offered either (1) a lump sum of $85,000 or (2) reimbursement of specified cost categories; jury awarded approximately $76,000.

### D. Evaluability, ambiguity, and the flexibility counter-current

**Berg v. Darden (2004) 120 Cal.App.4th 721.** Reported for the offeree-side standard: a § 998 offer's terms must be sufficiently specific to permit the offeree "to evaluate it and make a reasoned decision whether to accept it, or reject it and bear the risk he may have to shoulder his opponent's litigation costs and expenses." Reported on the flexible side: the offer **satisfied** § 998's specificity requirement notwithstanding that it did not identify the method by which the litigation would finally be resolved.
- **Service caveat.** Search results confirm that the appellant's opponent in Berg **also argued the offer "was not properly served,"** and that the trial court found the offer ineffective. **I could not determine from search results how the Court of Appeal resolved the service question, or the ultimate disposition.** Because Berg is the one located California case where a § 998 service challenge was squarely raised, **counsel should pull and read Berg in full before relying on it in either direction.**
- **Pin cite not confirmed.** (Secondary sources cite the evaluability standard to approximately p. 727; *reported*, not confirmed.)
- **Tag:** [CITE CORROBORATED / HOLDING PARTIALLY SUMMARIZED — SERVICE HOLDING UNRESOLVED]. **URLs indexed:** `https://www.casemine.com/judgement/us/5914b706add7b0493477ca12`; `https://underwood.law/blog/a-guide-to-statutory-california-settlement-offers-code-of-civil-procedure-section-998/`.

**Prince v. Invensure Ins. Brokers, Inc. (2018)**, Cal. Ct. App. 4th Dist., Div. 3, No. G051996. Secondary sources report the reporter citation as **23 Cal.App.5th 614**; *that reporter citation was reported by a single source and is not independently corroborated.* Reported holdings:
- The **validity of a § 998 offer is reviewed de novo**; **ambiguity in the offer is interpreted against its proponent**.
- Nevertheless, **contemporaneous written clarification** (there, emails) may resolve an ambiguity in a § 998 offer: "where two sophisticated parties are represented by counsel, allowing an offer to compromise to be clarified in writing after the offer was made serves the purpose of section 998." The trial court's ruling that the first offer was fatally ambiguous was reversed.

**On the "substantial compliance" framing generally.** I searched specifically for California authority applying a substantial-compliance doctrine to § 998 and **did not locate a case so holding.** The consistent report across practitioner sources is that § 998's *form* requirements are enforced strictly, while *interpretive* questions (ambiguity, scope of a release, whether the offer specified enough to be evaluated) are resolved with ordinary construction tools that can favor validity. **Counsel should treat "strict vs. substantial compliance" as a false binary on this record**, and should independently search for substantial-compliance authority in Westlaw before assuming none exists — my inability to locate it through a public search index is weak evidence.

### E. Timing, the acceptance window, and § 1013

**Poster v. Southern Cal. Rapid Transit Dist. (1990) 52 Cal.3d 266; 276 Cal.Rptr. 321; 801 P.2d 1072 (decided Dec. 24, 1990).** Two reported holdings:
1. A § 998 offer **is not revoked by a counteroffer** and may be accepted until expressly withdrawn by the offeror or deemed withdrawn under § 998 — contrary to the general contract rule.
2. **When a § 998 offer is served by mail, CCP § 1013 applies and extends the 30-day acceptance period by five days**, i.e., the offeree has at least **35 days** to accept. The reported reasoning: § 998 is not part of a specific procedural scheme conflicting with § 1013; both sit among the Code's miscellaneous provisions and apply generally to civil cases.
- **Pin cite not confirmed.** Secondary sources repeatedly cite the § 1013 holding to **p. 275**; that page number is *reported by secondary sources only* and must not be used as a pin cite without reading the opinion.
- **⚠ MATERIAL CONFLICT IN THE SEARCH RECORD — COUNSEL MUST RESOLVE.** Some search results describe **the Court of Appeal in Poster** as having held that "section 1013, subdivision (a) … **does not apply** to section 998 offers, and therefore did not serve to extend the time for acceptance," and as having found the acceptance untimely and reversed on that basis. The most coherent reading of the whole search record is that the Court of Appeal so held and the **Supreme Court reversed**, adopting the +5-day rule that every practitioner source now states as settled law. **But I could not read the opinion or its disposition line, and I cannot confirm which court held what.** This is precisely the kind of point that must be verified before it appears in a brief.
- **Tag:** [CITE CORROBORATED / HOLDING SUMMARIZED — INTERNAL CONFLICT UNRESOLVED]. **URLs indexed:** `https://law.justia.com/cases/california/supreme-court/3d/52/266.html`; `https://scocal.stanford.edu/opinion/poster-v-southern-cal-rapid-transit-dist-31238`.

**When the offer is "made."** Practitioner sources uniformly state that the § 998(b)(2) 30-day clock runs from the date the offer is **served**, and that each offer creates its own cost-shifting period from its service date. **No case was located squarely holding that the service date rather than a date printed on the offer controls**, and **no authority whatsoever was located addressing an *undated* § 998 offer.** § 998 as retrieved contains no express requirement that the offer bear a date.

### F. Burden of proof

**Licudine v. Cedars-Sinai Medical Center (2019) 30 Cal.App.5th 918.** The cleanest located statement of the two-part allocation. Reported: "**although the party making a 998 offer generally has the burden of showing that her offer is valid, it is the 998 offeree who bears the burden of showing that an otherwise valid 998 offer was not made in good faith.**" Reported three-factor good-faith framework: (1) how far into the litigation the offer was made; (2) the information available to the offeree before the offer expired; and (3) whether the offeree told the offeror it lacked sufficient information to evaluate the offer, and how the offeror responded. On the facts, an offer made 19 days after service of the complaint and 5 days after the answer was reasonably rejected as premature.
- **Pin cite not confirmed.**
- **Tag:** [CITE CORROBORATED / HOLDING SUMMARIZED]. **URLs indexed:** `https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/2019/b286350.html`; `https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/1973973.html`; `https://www.leagle.com/decision/incaco20190103023`.

**Elrod v. Oregon Cummins Diesel, Inc. (1987) 195 Cal.App.3d 692; 241 Cal.Rptr. 108.** Reported: "the Legislature intends that only good faith settlement offers qualify as valid offers under section 998"; where the offeror obtains a judgment more favorable than its offer, that judgment is **prima facie evidence** the offer was reasonable and the **burden shifts to the offeree** to prove otherwise; the offer must have been reasonable when made, judged on the information then available to the offeree. Addresses "token" offers.
- **Pin cite not confirmed.**
- **Tag:** [CITE CORROBORATED / HOLDING SUMMARIZED]. **URLs indexed:** `https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/3d/195/692.html`; `https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/2126177/elrod-v-oregon-cummins-diesel-inc/` (URL indexed but **the page itself was blocked and not read**).

**Nelson v. Anderson (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 111.** Reported: a prevailing party who made a valid § 998 offer is eligible for specified costs so long as the offer was reasonable and made in good faith; reasonableness/good faith is committed to the trial court's sound discretion; where a party prevails, its offer is presumed reasonable and the burden is on the party who rejected it to show costs should be disallowed. On the facts, the trial court taxed all § 998 costs on the ground the offer was a "token" offer.
- **Pin cite not confirmed.**
- **Tag:** [CITE CORROBORATED / HOLDING SUMMARIZED]. **URLs indexed:** `https://law.justia.com/cases/california/court-of-appeal/4th/72/111.html`; `https://caselaw.findlaw.com/court/ca-court-of-appeal/1224290.html`.

## Application to Our Facts

*Every statement in this section is framed as something counsel may consider or should evaluate. Nothing here predicts what any court will do, and nothing here should be read as a prediction that any argument will succeed or fail.*

### 1. Counsel should first re-anchor the analysis on the correct subdivision

Counsel may consider that, because our client is the **plaintiff/offeror**, the tasking's focus on § 998(c)(1) is misdirected: (c)(1) by its terms addresses "an offer made by a defendant." Counsel should evaluate the exposure and the upside under **§ 998(d)** instead, which is **discretionary** ("may require") and limited to **post-offer expert-witness fees** — meaning that even a flawlessly drafted offer would have purchased a discretionary remedy, not an automatic one. Counsel may consider that this materially changes the cost-benefit of litigating the offer's validity at all. Counsel should further evaluate whether **Civil Code § 3291** interest is available; on the retrieved text it is confined to actions "to recover damages for personal injury," and counsel may consider that a trust and real-property dispute is unlikely to qualify — which would remove the largest potential payoff from a plaintiff-side 998 offer.

### 2. Counsel should audit the offer against each statutory element before anything else

Counsel should evaluate the offer document against § 998(b)(1)'s elements one at a time: (i) writing; (ii) service on each offeree; (iii) service not less than 10 days before commencement of trial or arbitration; (iv) a statement of the offer containing the terms and conditions of the judgment; and (v) **a provision allowing the accepting party to indicate acceptance by signing a statement that the offer is accepted.** Counsel may consider that element (v) deserves immediate confirmation from the document itself: the facts as reported describe a "request to enter judgment" with a blank date line, which is not necessarily the same thing as a statutory acceptance provision. Counsel should evaluate that, under **Puerta** and **Mostafavi** as summarized above, the absence of a conforming acceptance provision is reported to be fatal to validity — and under Mostafavi, reported to render even a judgment entered on an acceptance void. Counsel may consider that this is a defect no argument about prejudice would reach, because on the reported reasoning there would have been "nothing to accept."

### 3. Counsel should treat the appraiser-valuation structure as the primary exposure

Counsel should evaluate the offer's operative content: a judgment requiring defendants to employ Stanley Xavier of Correia-Xavier for real property appraisal and Moss Adams for partnership appraisal within 30 days, valuing defendants' interests as of Hazel Leal's date of death, "with discounts as determined by those appraisers," for purposes of sale to plaintiff. Counsel may consider that under **Barella** as summarized, non-monetary terms do not invalidate an offer *per se*, but must be "sufficiently certain and capable of valuation to allow the court to determine whether judgment is more favorable than the offer." Counsel should evaluate the argument that this offer supplies no ascertainable value **at the time the offer was made**, because the entire economic content — the valuations *and* the discounts — is expressly delegated to third parties who had not yet been engaged and had not yet performed. Counsel may consider that **Gorobets**, decided August 6, 2026, is reported to require that at least one set of terms be "sufficiently certain to permit an accurate valuation **at the time the offer is made**," and should evaluate whether an offer whose number would exist only *after* acceptance and *after* a future appraisal process can satisfy that formulation. Counsel should also evaluate the structural parallel to **Sanford**: an offer whose real terms are deferred to a future process not set out in the offer.

Counsel may consider the countervailing argument that appraisal is a routine, objective valuation method, that the **subject matter** (real property and a partnership interest) is exactly the kind of asset appraisers value as a matter of course, and that this distinguishes the offer from **Barella**'s confidentiality condition, whose value was "highly subjective and elusive." Counsel should evaluate the practitioner commentary framing equity in real property as capable of valuation for § 998 purposes. Counsel may also consider that this argument is in tension with Gorobets's reported temporal framing — capable of valuation *when made* — and should evaluate whether the presence of an undetermined "discount" element (marketability, minority interest, or otherwise) is what pushes the offer from "valuable by an objective method" into "speculative."

### 4. Counsel should evaluate whether the $5,705,257.43 figure is part of the offer at all

Counsel may consider that the monetary sum appears only in the cover letter and not in the § 998 offer, and should evaluate whether that sum can be treated as an alternative term of the offer. Counsel should evaluate **Gorobets** on both sides of this: it is reported to *permit* alternative-terms offers, which helps, but it is reported to condition validity on the offer **"clearly present[ing] the alternatives"** — and counsel should evaluate whether a figure stated in a transmittal letter, absent from the offer document, clearly presents an alternative *within the offer*. Counsel may consider **Prince** as the best available support for reaching outside the four corners: it is reported to permit contemporaneous written clarification between sophisticated represented parties. Counsel should evaluate the limit of that argument — that Prince is reported to have resolved an **ambiguity** in an existing offer rather than to have imported a **term** that the offer omitted — and should evaluate whether importing a dollar figure is characterization or addition. Counsel may consider that if the letter's figure *is* treated as part of the offer, it could supply the certain, valuable alternative that Gorobets requires; and that if it is not, the offer is left with only the appraiser structure discussed above.

Counsel should also evaluate the interaction with **Prince**'s reported de novo standard of review and its reported rule that **ambiguity is construed against the proponent** — which here is our client.

### 5. Counsel should evaluate the proof-of-service defects as three distinct problems

**(a) The January 3, 2018 dates.** Counsel should evaluate that the POS recites both service and execution on a date more than eight years before the reported actual service date of 7/9/2026. Counsel may consider that this fails **CCP § 1013a**'s business-practice requirement of "the date and place of business where the correspondence was placed for deposit," because the date stated is not the date of deposit. Counsel should evaluate the compounding effect: because the offer itself is **undated**, the POS is the only intrinsic evidence of when the offer was "made" for § 998(b)(2) purposes — and on the face of the papers, an offer "made" January 3, 2018 would have been deemed withdrawn roughly 30 to 35 days later, in early 2018. Counsel should evaluate the risk that opposing counsel frames the offer as facially expired on its own documents.

**(b) The missing street address.** Counsel should evaluate that the envelope address block names the attorneys and firm but contains no street address, which appears to fail § 1013a's requirement of "the name and **address** of the person served as shown on the envelope," and is in tension with § 1013(a)'s requirement that the item be "addressed to the person on whom it is to be served, at the office address as last given by that person on any document filed in the cause."

**(c) Whether these are validity defects or evidentiary defects.** Counsel should evaluate the two available characterizations. On the adverse framing, counsel may consider that **service is a statutory element** of § 998(b)(1) ("any party may **serve** an offer in writing"), and that under the bright-line approach of Puerta and Mostafavi a failure to establish statutory service is not excused by intent or by the offeree's actual knowledge. On the favorable framing, counsel may consider the general principle — surfaced in search results but, importantly, **without a confirmable California citation** (see Authority § G) — that the **fact** of service and the **proof** of service are distinct, that a defective affidavit does not by itself undo a service that actually occurred, and that a proof of service creates a rebuttable presumption that may be impeached or supplemented by other evidence. Counsel should evaluate whether that principle can be located and cited in verified form before it is relied on, and should treat it as unsupported until it is.

**(d) Curative proof.** Counsel should evaluate what independent, admissible evidence exists that service actually occurred on 7/9/2026 — the mailing clerk's or paralegal's percipient declaration, calendar and mailroom records, postage-meter or docketing entries, the transmittal email if any, and any acknowledgment, response, or reference to the offer by opposing counsel. Counsel may consider that under Licudine our side bears the burden of showing validity, so this evidence would need to be assembled proactively rather than in reply. Counsel should evaluate whether an amended or corrected proof of service is available and appropriate, and what disclosure obligations attach to filing one.

### 6. Counsel should evaluate the undated offer separately from the POS

Counsel may consider that **§ 998 as retrieved contains no express requirement that the offer bear a date**, and that **no authority was located holding an undated § 998 offer invalid for that reason alone**. Counsel should therefore evaluate the argument that the blank "Dated: ______" lines on both the offer and the request to enter judgment are not independently fatal. Counsel should simultaneously evaluate that the absence of a date removes the one internal cross-check that would have exposed the 2018 POS date as a clerical error, and that the two defects are more damaging together than either is alone. Counsel may consider that this is the point at which the "non-prejudicial scrivener's error" argument is strongest in substance — a January 3, 2018 execution date on a 2026 offer is facially impossible and would have been understood as such by any reader — and weakest in form, because our side must still carry the burden of proving when the offer was made.

### 7. Counsel should evaluate the 30-day versus 35-day mismatch

Counsel should evaluate that the offer was served by mail and that, if **Poster** holds what secondary sources uniformly report, § 1013 extended the acceptance period to **35 days**, while our cover letter states the offer "shall remain open for a period of thirty (30) days." Counsel may consider two competing readings: that the letter merely restates the statutory default and is harmless surplusage; or that it purports to close the offer five days before the statute would, which opposing counsel may characterize as a non-conforming offer or as having misstated the offeree's rights. Counsel should evaluate whether any located authority permits an offeror to set a shorter acceptance period than § 998 provides — **none was located either way** — and should treat this as genuinely open. Counsel may consider that this point may matter little in practice if no acceptance was tendered at any point, and should evaluate whether it is worth briefing at all.

**Counsel must independently verify the Poster holding before relying on the 35-day figure.** As set out in Authority § E, the search record contains an unresolved conflict about whether the +5-day rule is the Supreme Court's holding or the Court of Appeal's rejected position.

### 8. Counsel should evaluate the burden allocation as it actually falls on our side

Counsel may consider that **Licudine** places the burden of establishing the offer's **validity** on the **offeror** — our client — meaning our side must affirmatively establish service, timing, form, and (given the non-monetary structure) valuability. Counsel should evaluate that this is the least favorable posture in which to litigate a defect-laden offer. Counsel may consider that the **good-faith** burden runs the other way under Licudine, and that under **Elrod** and **Nelson**, if our client ultimately obtains a judgment more favorable than the offer, the offer would be presumptively reasonable with the burden on defendants to show otherwise — but counsel should evaluate that this presumption is only reached **after** validity is established, and never rescues an invalid offer.

Counsel should also evaluate the Licudine good-faith factors prospectively: how far into the litigation this offer was made, what information defendants had when it expired, and whether defendants communicated that they lacked information to evaluate an appraisal-based offer and how our side responded. Counsel may consider preserving any correspondence bearing on that third factor.

### 9. Counsel should evaluate cure by re-service

Counsel may consider that the most direct response to nearly every defect above is to serve a **new, clean § 998 offer**: dated; containing an express statutory acceptance provision; stating a sum certain or, if alternatives are used, clearly presenting them within the offer document itself in the manner Gorobets is reported to require; and accompanied by a correct proof of service reciting the true mailing date and a complete address. Counsel should evaluate the § 998(b)(1) ten-day-before-trial cutoff and the current trial setting before doing so. Counsel may consider that a new offer does not retroactively repair the first one, and should evaluate what, if anything, is preserved by continuing to assert the July 2026 offer. Counsel should evaluate the reported effect of successive offers on when cost-shifting benefits vest — noting that **this point was not researched or verified for this memo**.

### 10. Counsel should evaluate the adverse brief before it is filed

Counsel may consider that opposing counsel (Nikole/Niki Cunningham, Whitney Thompson & Jeffcoach) has an unusually clean set of attacks available and should evaluate each in advance: (i) no ascertainable value at the time of the offer, under Barella and Gorobets; (ii) facial expiration in 2018 on our own proof of service; (iii) non-compliance with § 1013a as to both date and address; (iv) the monetary alternative living outside the offer document; (v) if applicable, absence of a conforming acceptance provision under Puerta and Mostafavi; and (vi) de novo review with ambiguity construed against our client under Prince. Counsel should evaluate which of these our side can neutralize with evidence, which require legal argument, and whether the aggregate risk justifies pressing the offer at all given that the only realistic recovery is discretionary expert fees under § 998(d).

**What is now VERIFIED (read in full via CourtListener, 8/18/2026):**

*Martinez v. Brownco Construction Co.* (2013) **56 Cal.4th 1014** [157 Cal.Rptr.3d 558, 301
P.3d 1167] (Baxter, J.) — CourtListener cluster 899532. Verbatim from the opinion:

> "The terms of section 998 do not prohibit a party from making more than one settlement
> offer, but they are silent as to the effect of a party's multiple offers. In this action,
> we consider whether a later offer extinguishes a previous offer for purposes of section
> 998's cost-shifting provisions. We conclude that where, as here, a plaintiff makes two
> successive statutory offers, and the defendant fails to obtain a judgment more favorable
> than either offer, allowing recovery of expert fees incurred from the date of the first
> offer is consistent with section 998's language and best promotes the statutory purpose to
> encourage settlements."

The opinion identifies (and engages, rather than mechanically applying) the two prior
appellate approaches: *Wilson v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.* (1999) 72 Cal.App.4th 382 — the
trial court below had relied on it for "[t]he most recently rejected offer is the only
pertinent offer. All prior offers are extinguished by the subsequent offer" — and
*Distefano v. Hall* (1968) 263 Cal.App.2d 380 (two defense offers under former § 997). It
also restates *T. M. Cobb Co. v. Superior Court* (1984) 36 Cal.3d 273: general contract
principles (e.g., Civ. Code § 1586 revocability) govern § 998 offers **only when** applying
them "best promote[s]" the settlement policy — under *T. M. Cobb*, a § 998 offer **is
revocable prior to acceptance or statutory expiration**.

**UNVERIFIED / for counsel:** subsequent history and citing treatment of all four cases
(CourtListener cannot KeyCite); whether *Martinez*'s first-offer rule extends beyond its
facts (plaintiff's two offers, judgment worse than both, expert fees) to an **offeror whose

involved escalating offers; ours would be de-escalating — the equities the Court stressed
