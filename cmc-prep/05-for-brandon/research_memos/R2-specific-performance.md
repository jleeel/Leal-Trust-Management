## Issue

1. What must a plaintiff establish to obtain specific performance in California under Civil Code sections 3384, 3390, and 3391 — inadequacy of the legal remedy, definite and certain terms, adequate consideration, mutuality of remedy, and ability to perform?
2. May a contractual **buyout** provision in a partnership agreement or a settlement agreement be specifically enforced, and is a partnership or LLC interest treated as sufficiently "unique" that damages are inadequate?
3. Where a contract requires the parties to **employ an appraiser** or to submit to an appraisal/valuation formula, is that term sufficiently certain to be specifically enforceable, or is it an unenforceable agreement to agree? Will a California court supply a valuation method — or fix value itself — when the designated appraiser is unavailable or a party refuses to cooperate?
4. Are appraisal provisions treated as **arbitration** under Code of Civil Procedure section 1280 et seq., such that the enforcement vehicle becomes a petition to compel rather than a specific-performance decree?
5. In the **trust** context, may a buy-sell or option be specifically enforced against a trustee, and can a co-trustee be compelled to perform?
6. How does the Civil Code section 3387 presumption that land is unique apply to a **tenancy-in-common** interest?
7. What defenses — unclean hands, laches, uncertainty, lack of mutuality, impossibility — bear on the claim?

## Short Answer

**On the elements.** California has a codified specific-performance scheme. Civil Code section 3384 authorizes the remedy generally; section 3390 lists what cannot be specifically enforced (including, at subdivision (e), an agreement whose terms "are not sufficiently certain to make the precise act which is to be done clearly ascertainable"); section 3391 bars enforcement against a party who received inadequate consideration or as to whom the contract is not just and reasonable; and section 3392 bars enforcement in favor of a party who has not performed his own conditions precedent. Statutory text for each was retrieved verbatim and appears below.

**On mutuality.** The classical mutuality-of-remedy requirement has been displaced by statute. Civil Code section 3386, as amended in 1969, permits specific performance "[n]otwithstanding that the agreed counterperformance is not or would not have been specifically enforceable," provided the remedy is otherwise appropriate and the counterperformance has been substantially performed or is assured or securable. Search-verified case law (Ellis v. Mihelis (1963) 60 Cal.2d 206) reflects the related principle that a plaintiff suing for specific performance of a real-property sale satisfies mutuality by submitting himself to the court's jurisdiction.

**On agreements to appraise — the central issue.** The research located directly on-point California authority, and the answer is favorable to enforceability in principle. Civil Code section 1613 provides that where a contract prescribes an *exclusive* method of ascertaining consideration that is possible on its face but in fact becomes impossible, **only that provision is void** — the contract survives. In **Bewick v. Mecham (1945) 26 Cal.2d 92**, a lease option priced "at a price and terms then to be agreed on ... and if not agreed on then to be fixed by arbitration" was enforced after the lessor refused to appoint an arbitrator; the trial court fixed the price and decreed specific performance, and the judgment was affirmed. **Petrolink, Inc. v. Lantel Enterprises** (2018) 21 Cal.App.5th 375 and (2022) 81 Cal.App.5th 156 reflect the same modern practice: on a lease option to purchase at "fair market value" by appraisal, where the parties' competing appraisals diverged sharply, the trial court appointed an independent appraiser, found value, and ordered specific performance. Civil Code section 1611 supplies a default measure — reasonable worth — where a contract does not determine consideration or leaves it to an interested party's discretion. Together these indicate that a valuation *mechanism* is materially different from an agreement to agree (contrast Bustamante v. Intuit, Inc. (2006) 141 Cal.App.4th 199), because the price is ascertainable by an objective process without any further assent from the parties.

**But there is a real countercurrent specific to this complaint's prayer.** The relief sought is an order that defendants **employ two named third parties** — Stanley Xavier of Correia-Xavier and Moss Adams. Civil Code section 3390(b) bars specific enforcement of "[a]n obligation to employ another in personal service," and section 3390(d) bars specific enforcement of "[a]n agreement to procure the act or consent of ... any other third person." Neither named appraiser can be compelled by this decree to accept the engagement. Counsel should expect that framing, and should note that Bewick, Civil Code section 1613, and Evidence Code section 730 all point toward a fallback in which the **court** determines value (or appoints its own appraiser) rather than commanding defendants to hire particular professionals.

**On appraisal-as-arbitration.** Code of Civil Procedure section 1280(a) expressly defines "Agreement" for purposes of the California Arbitration Act to "include[], but [not be] limited to, agreements providing for **valuations, appraisals, and similar proceedings**." Search-verified case law confirms courts treat appraisals as arbitrations (Kacha v. Allstate Ins. Co. (2006) 140 Cal.App.4th 1023; Lambert v. Carneghi (2008) 158 Cal.App.4th 1120; Louise Gardens of Encino Homeowners' Assn., Inc. v. Truck Ins. Exchange, Inc. (2000) 82 Cal.App.4th 648) — though **all located cases arise in the insurance context**, and no published non-insurance California decision was located in this session. This cuts both ways: it may expose the specific-performance claim to a motion to compel arbitration, and it simultaneously supplies a powerful cure for non-cooperation, because section 1281.6 directs that "if the agreed method fails or for any reason cannot be followed," the court "shall appoint the arbitrator" on petition.

**On the trust context.** Probate Code section 15620 makes a power vested in two or more trustees exercisable only by unanimous action unless the instrument provides otherwise — so relief likely must run against **both** co-trustees. Section 16013 imposes on each co-trustee a duty to participate in administration. Sections 850 and 856 authorize a petition and an order directing a fiduciary to execute a conveyance where a decedent was bound by a written contract to convey and the contract "is one which can be specifically enforced," and sections 17200 and 17206 give the probate court broad power over the internal affairs of a trust and to "make any orders and take any other action necessary or proper."

**On real property.** Civil Code section 3387's presumption that a breach of an agreement to transfer real property cannot be adequately relieved by money is **conclusive only for a single-family dwelling the plaintiff intends to occupy**; in all other cases — including 652.02 acres of agricultural land — it is only "a presumption affecting the burden of proof." No California authority applying section 3387 specifically to an undivided tenancy-in-common interest was located.

**On the discount question.** Genuinely two-sided; analyzed in the Application section below. In short: leaving discounts to appraiser discretion *helps* certainty by supplying a mechanism that requires no further party assent (sections 1611, 1613; Bewick; Petrolink), and *hurts* certainty to the extent section 3390(e) requires "the precise act which is to be done" to be "clearly ascertainable" and the aggregate discount is large enough to put Civil Code section 3391's "adequate consideration" and "just and reasonable" limits in play.

*Holding as described by search sources:* A lease contained an option to purchase "at a price and terms then to be agreed on between the parties hereto, and if not agreed on then to be fixed by arbitration, each of the parties hereto selecting one arbitrator, and the two selected a third." ### C. Mutuality and adequacy of consideration

## Application to Our Facts

*(Framed throughout as considerations. No outcome is predicted.)*

### 1. Framing the elements

Counsel may consider organizing the specific-performance showing around the statutory scheme rather than a common-law element list, because Civil Code sections 3384, 3386, 3387, 3390, 3391, and 3392 supply the operative rules and each has been verified verbatim above. Counsel should evaluate whether the operative pleading affirmatively alleges each statutory predicate — an enforceable written obligation, inadequacy of the legal remedy, certainty of terms under section 3390(e), adequacy of consideration and substantive fairness as to the defendant co-trustees under section 3391, and plaintiff's own performance or excuse under section 3392. Counsel should evaluate whether plaintiff's ability and willingness to pay the eventual appraised, discounted price is pleaded with the specificity section 3392 contemplates, and whether a tender allegation (or an allegation excusing tender because the price is not yet ascertained) is present. Counsel may consider that section 3386 removes the classical mutuality objection as a freestanding bar, and that Ellis v. Mihelis (if it reads as the search sources describe) supplies the complementary proposition that a plaintiff seeking specific performance of a real-property sale submits himself to the court's jurisdiction.

### 2. The three instruments and which one supplies the obligation

Counsel should evaluate, instrument by instrument, whether the December 7, 2017 Settlement Agreement and Mutual Release, the Tenants-in-Common Agreement, or the Manuel C. Leal & Son Dairy Partnership Agreement is the source of the buyout right, because the certainty analysis, the arbitration analysis, and the trust-vehicle analysis may each come out differently depending on which document controls. Counsel may consider that a claim resting on the 2017 Settlement Agreement invites reliance on the principle (reflected in Tamarind, subject to counsel reading it) that a settlement agreement may be specifically enforced where damages are inadequate. Counsel should evaluate whether the 2017 Settlement Agreement was made in a then-pending action and, if so, whether the court retained jurisdiction under Code of Civil Procedure section 664.6 — noting the verified text limits that vehicle to pending litigation and expressly carves out actions brought under the Probate Code at subdivision (c), and noting the section was repealed and re-added operative January 1, 2025, so the correct version must be identified. Counsel should evaluate whether the instruments contain their own arbitration, mediation, or dispute-resolution clauses, since none of the instruments were reviewed for this memo and such a clause would materially change the enforcement vehicle.

### 3. The certainty question and the "discounts as determined by the appraisers" problem — both sides

Counsel may consider that the strongest framing of plaintiff's position is that the instruments do not contain an agreement to agree but a **completed agreement with a valuation mechanism**, because the price is ascertainable by an objective process — appraisal by identified professionals as of a fixed date — without any further assent from either party. Counsel may consider that Civil Code section 1611 supports this framing directly, since it supplies "so much money as the object of the contract is reasonably worth" where a contract does not determine consideration, does not supply a method, **or leaves the amount to the discretion of an interested party** — and counsel should evaluate the argument that an independent appraiser is precisely *not* an interested party, so the clause is better than the situation section 1611 was written to rescue. Counsel may consider that Civil Code section 1613 and Bewick v. Mecham together supply the answer to non-cooperation: where an exclusive valuation method appears possible but becomes impossible in execution, only that provision fails and the court may ascertain the consideration in place of the appraisers. Counsel may consider that Petrolink illustrates the modern application of that principle on facts structurally similar to ours — an option priced at appraised fair market value, irreconcilable competing appraisals, a court-appointed independent appraiser, a judicial value finding, and a specific-performance decree.

Counsel should evaluate the opposing side of this issue honestly, because it is genuinely two-sided. Counsel should evaluate that Civil Code section 3390(e) requires terms "sufficiently certain to make **the precise act which is to be done** clearly ascertainable," and that a defendant will argue the precise act is not ascertainable where the ultimate price depends on discounts committed to appraiser discretion rather than fixed in the instruments. Counsel should evaluate the argument, drawn from Blackburn v. Charnley as described in search sources, that a contract must be **more** specific to support specific performance than to support damages — a proposition that, if the opinion bears it out, is the defense's best certainty authority and should be confronted rather than ignored. Counsel should evaluate whether Bustamante v. Intuit is distinguishable on the ground that the Bustamante parties had never settled the identity of the controlling owner, funding, or compensation — i.e., open **deal** terms requiring further negotiation — whereas the present instruments arguably leave open only an **application** of an agreed mechanism.

Counsel should evaluate the specific tension created by the 2017 Xavier valuation, in which a **90% contractual buyout discount** and a **25% minority interest discount** were reportedly applied per our counsel's correspondence. Counsel should evaluate whether the 90% figure is a **contractual** discount fixed by the instruments (in which case it supports certainty, because it is not discretionary at all) or an appraiser-selected figure (in which case it is the defense's central indefiniteness exhibit). Counsel should evaluate whether the 25% minority-interest discount is the kind of professional judgment ordinarily committed to a qualified appraiser under recognized valuation standards, which may support the argument that the mechanism supplies an ascertainable answer even though it does not supply a number. Counsel should evaluate that the mixed provenance of the two discounts — one apparently contractual, one apparently appraiser-determined — may itself generate an ambiguity argument about which discounts the phrase "as determined by" the appraisers is meant to reach.

Counsel should evaluate the **fairness** consequence of the discount stack independently of certainty, because Civil Code section 3391 bars specific enforcement against a party who "has not received an adequate consideration" or as to whom the contract "is not ... just and reasonable." Counsel should evaluate that a 90% discount compounded with a 25% minority discount produces a price that is a small fraction of undiscounted value, and should anticipate a section 3391 defense framed as substantive unfairness to the co-trustees and, derivatively, to the trust beneficiaries. Counsel should evaluate that section 3391's inquiry is expressly directed at the position of the **resisting** party, so evidence about what Hazel J. Leal or the trust received in exchange in 2017 — including any mutual releases — may be central. Counsel may consider that Civil Code section 3389 forecloses one adjacent argument, since the presence of a liquidated-damages or penalty provision would not by itself defeat specific enforcement.

### 4. The "employ an appraiser" prayer specifically

Counsel should evaluate the risk that the prayer as framed — an order that defendants **employ** Stanley Xavier of Correia-Xavier and **employ** Moss Adams — runs into Civil Code section 3390(b), which bars specific enforcement of "[a]n obligation to employ another in personal service." Counsel should evaluate the parallel risk under Civil Code section 3390(d), which bars specific enforcement of "[a]n agreement to procure the act or consent of ... any other third person," because neither Mr. Xavier nor Moss Adams is before the Court and neither can be compelled by this decree to accept the engagement, to be available, or to perform on any timetable. Counsel should evaluate whether either named appraiser has confirmed availability and willingness to perform a date-of-death valuation, and whether either has a conflict, since Mr. Xavier's prior 2017 engagement may be argued either as continuity (favorable) or as prior alignment with one side (unfavorable).

Counsel may consider pleading and briefing an **alternative** framing that does not depend on compelling an employment relationship: an order declaring the buyout right, directing the parties to cooperate in a valuation, and — if the designated mechanism fails — asking the Court to determine value itself under Civil Code section 1613 and Bewick, or to appoint a neutral appraiser under Evidence Code section 730 as reportedly occurred in Petrolink. Counsel should evaluate that Evidence Code section 730's closing sentence preserves licensure requirements, so any court-appointed valuer of the real property should hold the appropriate California appraisal license. Counsel may consider that framing the relief as a judicial valuation rather than a compelled hiring also blunts the section 3390(b) and (d) objections without giving up the substance of the remedy.

### 5. Appraisal-as-arbitration — vehicle risk and vehicle opportunity

Counsel should evaluate whether the appraisal provisions in the Settlement Agreement, Tenants-in-Common Agreement, or Partnership Agreement fall within Code of Civil Procedure section 1280(a)'s definition of "Agreement," which by its verified terms "includes, but is not limited to, agreements providing for valuations, appraisals, and similar proceedings." Counsel should evaluate the downside: if the provision is an arbitration agreement, defendants may petition to compel under section 1281.2 and argue the specific-performance cause of action must be stayed or that the valuation dispute belongs before the appraisers, not the Court. Counsel should evaluate the waiver exposure that runs in both directions under section 1281.2(a), including whether plaintiff's filing of a civil complaint seeking specific performance could later be characterized as inconsistent with a right to compel, and whether defendants' litigation conduct could waive theirs.

Counsel may consider the corresponding upside, which is substantial: section 1281.6 provides that "if the agreed method fails or for any reason cannot be followed, or when an arbitrator appointed fails to act and his or her successor has not been appointed, the court, on petition of a party to the arbitration agreement, **shall** appoint the arbitrator," and prescribes the five-nominee procedure. Counsel should evaluate that this statutory route may be a cleaner and faster answer to a refusal to cooperate or to an appraiser's unavailability than a contested specific-performance trial, and may consider whether a petition to compel plus a section 1281.6 appointment request should be pleaded in the alternative. Counsel should evaluate Kacha's reported scope limitation — that appraisers, unlike arbitrators, may have materially narrower authority and may not resolve legal or coverage-type questions — and should consider whether the contested issues here (which instrument governs, whether a triggering event occurred, what the correct valuation date is, whether the 90% discount is contractual) are **legal** questions reserved to the Court even if pure valuation goes to appraisers. Counsel should evaluate that no located California case applies appraisal-as-arbitration outside the insurance context, so any brief on this point should rest primarily on the text of section 1280(a) and should candidly acknowledge the insurance provenance of Kacha, Lambert, and Louise Gardens.

### 6. Trust-specific considerations

Counsel should evaluate whether relief must be directed at **both** successor co-trustees, because Probate Code section 15620 makes a power vested in two or more trustees exercisable only by unanimous action unless the trust instrument provides otherwise — meaning an order binding only one co-trustee may be ineffective to convey. Counsel should evaluate whether the Hazel J. Leal Revocable Trust dated November 3, 2008 contains a contrary provision permitting majority or several action, since that would change the analysis. Counsel may consider Probate Code section 16013(a), which imposes on each co-trustee an affirmative duty to participate in administration, as support for the proposition that a co-trustee cannot defeat a trust obligation by simply declining to act, and section 16000, which requires administration according to the trust instrument.

Counsel should evaluate whether a Probate Code section 850 petition is a better or additional vehicle than a civil specific-performance complaint. Counsel should evaluate that section 850(a)(2)(A) and (B) are keyed to a **decedent** who while living was bound by a written contract to convey real property or transfer personal property and who died before conveying — which may map onto Hazel J. Leal's execution of the 2017 Settlement Agreement before her death — and that subdivision (a)(2)(B) expressly conditions relief on the contract being "one which can be specifically enforced," so the specific-performance analysis is imported wholesale. Counsel should evaluate the fit problem: subdivision (a)(2) speaks of the decedent and is available to "the personal representative or any interested person," while subdivision (a)(3), the trustee-facing provision, is drafted around claims *by* the trustee or creditor-of-settlor claims, and its subdivision (a)(3)(A) reaches property held by the trustee that "is claimed to belong to another" — a phrase counsel should evaluate for whether a contractual buyout right qualifies. Counsel may consider that Probate Code section 856 supplies the operative order — directing "the personal representative or **other fiduciary**" to execute a conveyance "or granting other appropriate relief" — which is a close statutory analogue to a specific-performance decree against a trustee. Counsel should evaluate Probate Code sections 17200 and 17206 as an alternative or supplemental route, while noting that section 17200(a) by its verified terms confers petition standing on "a trustee or beneficiary," so counsel should evaluate whether plaintiff qualifies as a beneficiary of this trust or must rely on section 850's broader "any interested person" language. Counsel should evaluate whether the matter belongs in, or should be coordinated with, the probate department of the Tulare County Superior Court, and whether any consolidation or related-case notice is appropriate.

### 7. The real property and the tenancy-in-common interest

Counsel may consider that Civil Code section 3387 supplies a presumption that breach of an agreement to transfer real property cannot be adequately relieved by money, and should evaluate that on these facts the presumption is the **rebuttable** kind — a presumption affecting the burden of proof — because 652.02 acres of agricultural land is not "a single-family dwelling which the party seeking performance intends to occupy." Counsel should evaluate that the practical effect is burden-shifting rather than conclusive, so defendants may attempt to prove that money would in fact make plaintiff whole. Counsel may consider marshalling the ordinary uniqueness proof anyway — the specific acreage, water rights, soil and irrigation characteristics, the dairy operation's dependence on the parcels, and the absence of a substitute — rather than resting on the presumption alone. Counsel should evaluate that no California authority applying section 3387 to an **undivided tenancy-in-common interest** was located in this session, and should therefore anticipate an argument that a fractional undivided interest is a financial rather than a possessory asset and is more readily monetized than a fee. Counsel may consider that Real Estate Analytics, LLC v. Vallas (if it reads as search sources describe) supports the proposition that refusing specific performance for a breached land sale can be an abuse of discretion, while also noting the search-reported wrinkle that Code of Civil Procedure section 405.33 makes section 3387's presumption inapplicable in a discrete lis pendens undertaking context — a limitation counsel should confirm before relying on the case.

### 8. The partnership interest

Counsel should evaluate that no published California authority holding a partnership or LLC interest to be "unique" for specific-performance purposes was located, and should not assert such a rule to the Court without independent verification. Counsel may consider building the inadequacy showing for the partnership interest on facts rather than a presumption — that the Manuel C. Leal & Son Dairy Partnership is a closely held family enterprise with no market for its interests, that the interest carries governance and control rights that money cannot replicate, and that the interest is functionally intertwined with the 652.02 acres. Counsel may consider Corporations Code section 16701 as a structural analogy rather than as direct authority: subdivision (i) expressly directs that "[t]he court shall determine the buyout price" of a dissociated partner's interest and authorizes the court to shift "the fees and expenses of appraisers or other experts" against a party who "acted arbitrarily, vexatiously, or not in good faith." Counsel should evaluate that this analogy answers a competence objection (courts do value partnership interests) but does not by itself supply the buyout right here, since section 16701 is a dissociation default that a partnership agreement may displace and this claim rests on the parties' own instruments. Counsel should evaluate whether any dissociation or dissolution event has occurred that would make section 16701 directly applicable in the alternative, and should evaluate the good-faith fee-shifting provision as potential leverage if defendants' non-cooperation is established.

Counsel should evaluate — because it is the most dangerous unaddressed issue surfaced by this research — whether the buyout was **triggered at all**, given that the unpublished Estate of Silveira decision reportedly held that the death of an original partner did not trigger a buy-sell where the partnership interest had already been transferred to a trust. Counsel should evaluate whether the Manuel C. Leal & Son Dairy partnership interest was transferred into the Hazel J. Leal Revocable Trust before Hazel Leal's death, and whether the governing buy-sell language keys to the death of a **partner**, of a **person**, or to a transfer event. Counsel should evaluate that Estate of Silveira is unpublished and cannot be cited by either side under California Rules of Court, rule 8.1115, but that the underlying interpretive question is one opposing counsel may raise on its own reasoning.

### 9. Defenses

Counsel should evaluate **uncertainty** under Civil Code section 3390(e) as the defense most likely to be pressed, and should prepare the section 1611 / section 1613 / Bewick answer in the moving papers rather than in reply. Counsel should evaluate **impossibility** in its precise statutory form: Civil Code section 1612 provides that where an exclusive valuation method is impossible **on its face**, the **entire contract is void**, while section 1613 saves the contract where the method was possible on its face but becomes impossible in fact. Counsel should evaluate which side of that line a clause naming two specific appraisers falls on if one or both are now unavailable, since naming particular individuals invites an argument that the method was always dependent on third-party willingness — and counsel should evaluate that the answer likely turns on whether the clause is read as requiring these appraisers exclusively or as designating them subject to substitution. Counsel should evaluate **lack of mutuality** as largely foreclosed by Civil Code section 3386 and by Ellis v. Mihelis, but should evaluate whether section 3386(b) is satisfied — i.e., whether plaintiff's counterperformance (payment of the discounted price) is "substantially performed or its concurrent or future performance is assured or ... can be secured to the satisfaction of the court" — and may consider offering to deposit funds or post security to remove the issue. Counsel should evaluate **unclean hands** and **laches** as fact-driven equitable defenses, and should specifically evaluate the elapsed time between the December 7, 2017 Settlement Agreement, Hazel Leal's death, and the filing of this action, together with any prejudice defendants may claim from valuation drift, changed agricultural conditions, or the death or unavailability of witnesses. Counsel should evaluate that a date-of-death valuation date partially neutralizes a laches-prejudice argument as to price, while potentially sharpening it as to evidence availability. Counsel should evaluate Civil Code section 3392 as an affirmative hurdle rather than a defense — plaintiff must have "fully and fairly performed all the conditions precedent" — and should identify every condition precedent in each instrument. Counsel should evaluate Civil Code section 3391(3) and (4) exposure if defendants contend Hazel Leal's 2017 assent was procured by misrepresentation or given under mistake, particularly given the 90% discount figure.

### 10. Enforcement of any decree

Counsel may consider that even a favorable decree requires an execution mechanism against a fiduciary who declines to sign, and should evaluate the elisor practice reflected in Blueberry Properties, LLC v. Chow, in which the court appointed the clerk of the court to sign escrow documents for a refusing seller. Counsel should evaluate the statutory basis for elisor appointment independently, because the search sources conflicted on the citation and it was not verified here. Counsel may consider that Civil Code section 3395 may matter if any interest in the subject property has been transferred since the obligation arose, since it extends enforceability to persons claiming under the obligor by a subsequently created title, excepting good-faith purchasers and encumbrancers for value.
