## The facts, plainly
Four dentists owned their office building through a partnership (1975). Their agreement said:
when a partner DIES, his estate has 90 days to elect to stay in; if it doesn't, the surviving
partners get 60 days to BUY the dead partner's share at a referee's valuation minus 7%. In 1994,
with everyone's written consent, Dr. Hallberg swapped himself out as partner and substituted
"Richard W. Hallberg, as Trustee of THE RICHARD W. HALLBERG TRUST" — his living trust — "in place
of RICHARD W. HALLBERG, individually." He died in 2010. The survivors pressed the buyout button:
"we exercise our option to purchase the interest of Dr. Richard Hallberg, deceased." His son (by
then sole successor trustee) answered: the partner isn't a dead man — the partner is the TRUST,
and the trust didn't die. No death of a partner, no buyout.

The trial court sided with the survivors (following *Presta*: the man, not the trust, is the
partner) and fixed a buyout at $723,366. The Court of Appeal REVERSED:

1. **A trust can be a partner.** The UPA defines "person" to include a "trust" (Corp. Code
   § 16101(9), (13)).
2. **The 1994 amendment expressly made the trustee-of-the-trust the partner** "in place of
   [Dr. Hallberg] individually" — "we will not pretend the substitution … did not happen."
3. **The trustee's death is not a dissociation.** Quoting § 16601(8): a trust-partner is
   dissociated by "distribution of the trust's entire transferable interest in the partnership,
   but NOT merely by reason of the substitution of a successor trustee." The buyout-on-death
   clause was keyed to the death of *a partner*; the partner (the trust) never died; the button
   was never validly pressed. The trust "continues to be a partner."

## Why defendants will wave this case at us
It is their best authority for the post-death profits claim: the Leal partner is
"THE HAZEL J. LEAL REVOCABLE TRUST" (Art. I.B, 37.5%); Hazel's death merely substituted Ashley
and Susan as trustees; under Han + § 16601(8), that is not a dissociation; ergo (they say) the
Trust is still a partner collecting 37.5% until the buyout closes.

## Why Han does not decide our case — the distinctions, honestly ranked

**1. The Leal agreement says what the dentists' agreement did not.** Han turned on contract
text: the buyout there was keyed generically to "the death of any partner," and the partner (the
trust) didn't die. Our Art. XIII.B.1 is keyed BY NAME to the human being: "If Hazel dissociates
from the Partnership … **as a result of Hazel's death** … (the 'Dissociating Events'), then the
Partnership **shall** purchase her Partnership Interest," and B.2: "**In the event of the death
of Hazel**, the Partnership shall continue … The Partnership shall purchase her Partnership
Interest at ninety percent (90%)…" — where "Hazel" is a defined party that includes "Trustee of
THE HAZEL J. LEAL REVOCABLE TRUST." **Han's own method — hold the parties to their written
words — is the argument for us.** The drafters in 2018 (post-Presta, post-Han-trial) chose the
person's death, not "death of a partner," as the trigger.

**2. Even under Han, the buyout obligation triggered.** Han's consequence was that an OPTIONAL
buyout was never triggered, so there was nothing to enforce. Ours is MANDATORY and expressly
triggered by Hazel's death — whether or not the Trust also "dissociated" for statutory purposes.
The live question is only the meantime economics: 37.5% profits (their view) or the contract's
4% note interest (Art. XIII.B.2; § 16701(b)'s interest-from-dissociation model per *Rappaport*).
Han says nothing about a trust-partner collecting profits while under a matured mandatory buyout.

**3. Han's equities ran the other way.** There, the trust side wanted to STAY a partner and keep
the asset (no buyout at all). Here, defendants want the buyout money AND partner profits AND 4%…
Han does not hold that combination; counsel may consider pressing the election.

## What cuts against us, honestly
- **Art. I.B names the Trust as the partner** — the same structural fact that decided Han.
- **§ 16601(8) is real, verified text** (quoted verbatim in Han and in *Rappaport*).
- **Our own books behave like Han's rule applies**: the FY2023–FY2025 statements keep allocating
  37.5% to the "Estate of Hazel Leal" and distributing to it (practical construction — see
  `../01-verified-facts/post-death-profits-issue.md` §5.4, §11).
- The 2018 drafters' "Dissociating Events" clause must do the work of contracting around the
  statutory default — **whether § 16601(8) is waivable by agreement (Corp. Code § 16103) is the
  question Brandon must run on Westlaw.** § 16103(a)'s default rule ("relations among the
  partners … are governed by the partnership agreement") was quoted in *Rappaport*; the
  non-waivable list in § 16103(b) was not verified here.

## One practical lesson worth telling the client
The dentists litigated for **nine years** — referee, two trial phases, consolidated appeals,
Supreme Court review — over a $723,366 buyout, and the survivors ended up with a reversed
judgment and costs against them. The dispute was ultimately about which piece of paper controls.
Ours says more than theirs did. That is an argument for resolving on the contract's own terms,
quickly.
